## Snapshot

- **Founded:** Usually dated to 1943 and the XP-80 jet fighter project at Lockheed's Burbank, California facility [1][2]. Some sources note "unofficial" origins with the P-38 in 1939 [2]; the 80th-anniversary framing in 2023 supports the 1943 date [2]. The name is attributed to the "Skonk Oil" factory in the Li'l Abner comic strip, but that account is described as legend [2][3].
- **Parent:** Lockheed Corporation, now Lockheed Martin Aeronautics; formally "Advanced Development Programs" (ADP) [4][5].
- **Location:** Originally Burbank, CA; today centered on Palmdale, CA [6][7]. A 215,000 sq ft "intelligent factory" opened there in 2021, with space for 450+ employees [6]. An older Valley business journal listing cited 3,200+ employees at Palmdale [7]; that figure is dated and total current headcount was not found.
- **Funding model:** Customer-funded government development (CIA, USAF, DARPA) under classified programs [8][9][10]. Early contracts were informal: work on the XP-80 began months before the formal contract arrived (16 October 1943) [11].
- **Status today:** Still operating within Lockheed Martin Aeronautics. A company spokesperson describes current work as next-generation unmanned systems, multi-domain operations, future fighters and hypersonics [6]. Programme-level detail on current work was not retrieved.

## Operating model

**Kelly's 14 Rules: what I could verify.** Lockheed Martin publishes the rules on its own site, but I could not retrieve that page [21]. Secondary sources say the rules began on the XP-80 in 1943 and were formalized in the early 1950s [21][22]; one blog dates them to 1954 citing Ben Rich's book [21]. A NATO AGARD paper credits Johnson with writing the "Basic Operating Rules" [23]. Wording varies between versions: later versions swap "customer" for "military" and "vendor" for "contractor" [24], and Jalopnik's "near complete control" differs from other lists' "practically complete control" [21][25].

Fragments confirmed in search results (not a full verbatim list):

- **Rule 1** gives the program manager practically complete control of the program, reporting to a division president or higher [21][25].
- **Rule 3** says the number of people connected with the project must be restricted "in an almost vicious manner" (wording shown in search results from Check-Six and Wikipedia) [24][26].
- **Rule 9** holds that the contractor should be allowed to test the final product in flight [21].
- The AGARD paper says the rules address program management, organization, contractor/customer relations, documentation, reporting, specifications, drawings, funding, cost control, subcontractor inspection, testing, security and management compensation [23].
- Johnson's motto was "Be quick, be quiet, be on time" [21].

**The remaining rules (2, 4-8, 10-14) are not listed here because I did not see their text.** To complete this section, copy them from Lockheed Martin's Skunk Works page or from Ben Rich's *Skunk Works* (Little, Brown, 1994) [unverified citation details].

**Operating features supported by sources:**

- **Small teams and authority:** a lead engineer fully responsible for decisions, tight-knit small teams, minimal oversight [11].
- **Informal contracting:** work began on a handshake; formal paperwork followed [11].
- **Security:** secrecy was a design condition (Rule 3 and the "be quiet" motto) [21][24]. The CIA delegated design and manufacturing entirely to Lockheed on the U-2 [27].
- **Co-location of design and manufacturing:** the XP-80 team worked in a rented tent next to the Burbank plant [1][12]. Wikipedia describes the enclosure as pallets and crates around a shack, covered by the tent [12]. Describing this as a deliberate co-location principle is my reading, not a quote from a source.
- **Customer interface:** a small number of customer representatives working directly with the program manager. The CIA's Richard Bissell ran the U-2 programme for the agency [14][27]. Beyond that, detailed customer-interface claims come from the rules text I could not retrieve.

## What made it work

*Evidence-backed:*

1. **Short, fixed-scope projects with clear customers.** The XP-80, U-2 and A-12 all had an urgent government buyer, and the U-2 and A-12 were responses to an identified threat [13][17].
2. **Delegated authority and a trusted customer.** Bissell "turned the design and manufacturing operation completely over to Lockheed" [27]; Rule 1 gives the program manager control [21].
3. **Speed against stated targets.** The XP-80 delivered in 143 days against a 150- or 180-day target [1][11]. These timelines come mostly from Lockheed-affiliated or popular accounts, so their independence is limited.
4. **Honest budget behaviour (anecdotal).** Johnson reportedly returned most of the money on the "Suntan" project when the technology proved out of reach [11].
5. **Technical leadership at the top.** Johnson, the designer, ran the shop; Rich later led stealth development [19][20].

*Inference (mine, not sourced):* the pairing of high technical leadership with a customer willing to accept informal contracting is probably what made the rules work, rather than any rule on its own.

## Limits / failure modes

- **Dependence on classified, special contracting conditions.** Informal starts and delegated authority relied on customers (CIA, USAF) with unusual autonomy and secrecy [11][27]. I did not retrieve any source examining contract types (cost-plus vs. fixed-price) and I will not assert them; this remains an open gap.
- **Cost claims are thinly documented.** "Under budget" for the U-2 comes from a hall-of-fame page [16] and "all aircraft under budget and on time" comes from supporters [11]; no independent audit was found. F-117 cost depends on what is counted, with a roughly 2.5x gap between flyaway and fully loaded cost [19].
- **Prototype attrition.** Both Have Blue demonstrators crashed [20]. Whether that reflects aggressive risk-taking or a cost is not assessed in the sources I saw.
- **Later struggles.** I found no source that I read on post-1990 programme overruns or schedule problems at Skunk Works; this is a gap, not evidence of none.
- **Copycats.** The one empirical source retrieved is Oliver and Cole's study of "TechPlus," a corporate skunkworks that succeeded in the market but was seen as an organizational failure; when the parent tried to reintegrate it, nearly all members left, and the follow-up group was kept closer to the parent and narrower in scope [28]. A critic argues skunkworks may deliver a product without making the parent more innovative, and may reduce that ability [28]. The "copy a startup with an isolated group" lesson is described as oversimplified [28]. This is one case, so it is weak evidence about copycats in general. I did not find a systematic study of success rates.

## Lessons for modern operating models

1. **Give one accountable leader real authority over scope, staffing and customer contact.** [Evidence: moderate] Rule 1 and the U-2 delegation support it [21][27]; no controlled comparison found.
2. **Keep the team deliberately small and restrict outside connections.** [Evidence: moderate] Rule 3 text is confirmed [24][26], but evidence that size caused success is anecdotal.
3. **Tie the work to a named customer with an urgent need and a fixed date.** [Evidence: moderate] The XP-80, U-2 and A-12 all fit [11][13][17]; the sample is small and survivor-biased.
4. **Staged proof before scale (Have Blue before F-117).** [Evidence: moderate] [19][20]
5. **Let work start before contract paperwork is finished, but only with a trusting sponsor.** [Evidence: weak] One documented case (XP-80) [11]; high risk outside defense. Mostly inference.
6. **Plan for reintegration from day one; an isolated unit can win the product and lose the organization.** [Evidence: weak to moderate] Single TechPlus case [28], plus my inference that Skunk Works avoided this largely because it was the sponsor's long-term supplier.
7. **Do not assume the model transfers without the funding and secrecy conditions.** [Evidence: inference] Based on the delegation and classified context above [11][27]; no direct transferability study found.

## Open questions / contested claims

- **XP-80 schedule:** 150-day vs 180-day deadline; 7-day vs 37-day margin [1][2][11].
- **U-2 "eight months":** go-ahead to first flight (about nine months by the dates above), or to delivery (September 1956)? Sources are inconsistent [13][14][15].
- **Rules' date and wording:** 1943, early 1950s or 1954; and "practically complete" vs "near complete" control [21][22][24][25].
- **The tent origin story** is repeated widely; its documentation is unclear [2][12].
- **F-117 cost** of $42-45M vs $111M depends on accounting [19].
- **Not established here:** Skunk Works contracting types, current headcount and post-1990 performance, the exact SR-71 timeline, and success rates of imitators.
