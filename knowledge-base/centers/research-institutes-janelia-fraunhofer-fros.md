# Research institutes and new-model orgs

**Scope and method note.** This profile compares Janelia (HHMI), Fraunhofer, the Allen Institute, the Broad Institute, Microsoft Research, Convergent Research's Focused Research Organizations (FROs), Arc Institute, UK ARIA and Wellcome Leap. During research, direct page fetching was blocked in this environment (DNS/egress policy denials), so every citation below rests on pages as surfaced in search-result summaries, not on full-text reads. Primary pages (Janelia, Fraunhofer, Nature) could not be opened; where a primary source was only seen in a search listing, the claim is attributed to what the listing said. Figures that conflict across sources are flagged. Statements labelled **Inference** are the author's, not sourced facts.

## Janelia Research Campus (HHMI)

### Key outputs
- The FlyEM team and Google released the hemibrain connectome in 2020: roughly half a fly brain, about 25,000 neurons and over 20 million connections, described as the largest synapse-resolution map in any species and released as a free public resource [7].
- The Azoulay et al. work evaluates HHMI's *investigator* program, not Janelia itself (see below). Do not attribute those results to Janelia's campus model. **Inference.**

### Operating model
- Janelia opened in Ashburn, Virginia, in 2006 as HHMI's first stand-alone research facility, modeled on AT&T Bell Labs and the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology; its profile describes a 15-year research model advancing one to three research areas at a time [3].
- Funding is institutional, so scientists do not write grants. The eLife retrospective puts Janelia at just under 15% of HHMI's annual operating budget [5].
- The HHMI investigator program, which funds people rather than projects, features long review cycles and tolerance for early failure. Azoulay, Graff Zivin and Manso compared HHMI investigators with NIH grantees using propensity-score weighting and difference-in-differences, and report a much higher rate of high-impact papers and shifts toward novel lines of inquiry [1]. A secondary summary reports twice as many top-5%-cited papers and three times as many top-1% papers versus early-career NIH prize winners, and 50% more top-1% papers versus NIH MERIT grantees [1].

### What worked
- Shared, open, team-built infrastructure (connectomics, tools) that single academic labs struggle to fund [7].
- Long horizons plus person-based selection are associated with more high-impact output in the HHMI comparison [1]. This is evidence about the investigator program; the Janelia campus model is a different, though related, design.

### Failure modes / unknowns
- Budget figures conflict ($80M at founding [4], $130M [6], ~$300M in third-party profiles); no audited recent figure was retrieved.
- Selection effects: HHMI picks promising scientists, and the authors address this with matching and DiD design [1], but residual confounding cannot be ruled out. **Inference.**
- I found no controlled evaluation of Janelia's 15-year, project-team model against alternatives. **Unknown.**

## Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft

### Key outputs
- 76 institutes and research units with nearly 32,000 employees [8]. (Audio/video compression and other widely known Fraunhofer technologies were not verified in the sources I could access and are therefore omitted.)

### Operating model
- Fraunhofer's own site says institutional funding is no more than about one-third of the budget, the rest coming from contracts [10]. Secondary descriptions give a three-way split: base funding, industry contracts, and competitive public grants [10][11]. Base funding is linked to success, the key indicator being economic return; institutes that cannot attract contracts shrink [10][11].
- Contract research was 83% of total volume in 2019 [8]. For 2024, one page reports EUR 3.4B total with EUR 3.0B contract research [8]; the IOF annual report reports EUR 3.6B with EUR 3.1B [9]. The 2024 breakdown is also given as 88% contract revenue and 12% federal/state funding [8], which uses a different denominator than the one-third framing; the sources are not directly comparable.

### What worked
- A built-in market test: revenue measures demand for applied research and disciplines institutes [10][11].
- Commentary attributes the model's success partly to Germany's thousands of medium-sized engineering firms as customers [search summary of policy analysis, see 12].

### Failure modes / unknowns
- Impact figures come from Fraunhofer-commissioned research, so they are contested [12 and related commentary].
- NCB argues revenue shows demand but not knowledge transfer, and that the model may route around university-industry collaboration [12].
- A commentary argues that public-project-heavy contract work gives cost advantages over private competitors [search summary, see 11/12].
- Replicability depends on a state willing to close or restructure weak institutes and on a dense SME base [search summary]. **Inference:** transplanting the model without those conditions is risky.

## Allen Institute

### Key outputs
- Began with the Allen Brain Atlas; open data was a founding choice, as Allen's team initially considered charging for database access before opting for free access [14].

### Operating model
- Paul Allen announced $100M in 2003; the institute started with four employees, three-year contracts, a small rented lab and one big project [13][14]. Later funding mixes government, foundation and private sources [13].

### What worked
- A single, bounded, shared-resource mission (atlas) with open release [14].

### Failure modes / unknowns
- Headcount sources disagree (about 825 vs 1,400). No current budget or independent outcome evaluation retrieved.

## Broad Institute

### Key outputs
- Not independently documented in the sources I accessed. Omitted rather than asserted.

### Operating model
- Launched in May 2004 after planning among the Broads, MIT, Whitehead, Harvard and affiliated hospitals; the founding gift was $100M over 10 years, doubled to $200M, then a $400M endowment in 2008 to make it permanent, totaling $600M from the Broads [15][16]. Stanley Medical Research Institute gave $100M in 2007 [16]. More than 1,200 scientists and staff are affiliated from Harvard and MIT [16].

### What worked
- A time-limited gift that proved itself and was then converted to an endowment is a clear funder pattern [15][16]. Whether that proves the institute's scientific effectiveness is a separate question. **Inference.**

### Failure modes / unknowns
- No operating budget or outcome analysis retrieved. The founding year is stated variously as 2003 and 2004 [15].

## Microsoft Research

### Key outputs
- Founded in 1991 by Rick Rashid, who led it to 2013, growing to more than 850 researchers across nearly a dozen labs, doing both basic and applied research [17].

### Operating model
- Corporate-funded lab with open publication culture and academic ties [17].

### What worked
- Longevity and scale across 30+ years [17]. Specific discoveries and award counts were not verified here.

### Failure modes / unknowns
- The 2014 closure of the Silicon Valley lab, part of 18,000 job cuts, drew an open letter from researchers originally signed by 28; the research chief reaffirmed fundamental research commitment [18]. Reported job losses vary (about 50 vs "dozens") [18][19].
- CACM places this in a pattern with Bell Labs, PARC and DEC labs; a blog argues Bell Labs' freedom depended on its parent's monopoly cash [18][19]. That is interpretation, not measured fact. **Inference:** corporate labs' autonomy tracks parent profitability and strategy.

## Convergent Research and Focused Research Organizations (FROs)

### Key outputs
- Portfolio per Convergent: E11 Bio (neural circuit mapping), Cultivarium (microbial synthetic biology tools), Lean FRO (Lean proof assistant for mathematicians), among others including Forest Neurotech (now Arbor Neuroscience), EvE Bio, Worthy, CHI FRO and ParallelSq; sources differ on the full roster [20][23].
- E11 Bio's PRISM pilot in mouse hippocampus claims nearly an order-of-magnitude tracing accuracy gain; the 100x cost reduction is a projection; E11 says substantial scale-up work remains [22]. These are the organization's own claims, not independent validation.

### Operating model
- Nonprofit "start-ups" with fixed lifespans for bottleneck problems; all Convergent FROs reportedly have a go/no-go milestone at two years [21]. Adam Marblestone is Convergent's CEO [32]. Forest Neurotech received a $14M commitment from Eric Schmidt in March 2024 [23]. Funders listed include Schmidt family, Hearst, Sloan, Simons and others [20].
- The Marblestone et al. Nature article proposing the model could not be opened; I describe the FRO concept only from Convergent and press sources.

### What worked
- Early technical milestones reported by organizations themselves (e.g. [22]). No independent outcome data.

### Failure modes / unknowns
- Too new for outcome evidence. Dependence on a small donor base and the question of what happens after the fixed term are open. **Inference.**

## Arc Institute

### Key outputs
- Arc Virtual Cell Atlas, launched in 2025 starting from over 300 million cells, with Tahoe-100M among datasets [26]; Evo genomic foundation models (described in secondary reports) [26].

### Operating model
- Nonprofit launched December 2021 with pledges above $650M, co-founded by Silvana Konermann, Patrick Hsu and Patrick Collison, partnered with Stanford, UC Berkeley and UCSF; core investigators get fully funded, renewable 8-year appointments, and affiliates remain at universities with partial Arc funding [24][25].

### What worked
- Fast visible outputs in computational biology [26]. Quality assessment is not available.

### Failure modes / unknowns
- Under four years old; no outcome evidence. Dependence on a few wealthy donors. **Inference.**

## ARIA (UK)

### Key outputs
- Second cohort of programme directors and new program areas announced April 2025 [33]. No outcome data retrieved.

### Operating model
- Backed with GBP 800M through 2025-26 [28][29], established January 2023; Ilan Gur, founder of Activate and ex-ARPA-E programme director, was named first CEO in 2022 after the first selection withdrew [29]. Wikipedia's infobox lists Kathleen Fisher as current CEO and a FY2024/25 budget of GBP 276M [27]; I could not confirm the transition details or dates.

### What worked
- Too early to say.

### Failure modes / unknowns
- Leadership turnover at the start [29]; critics (an opinion Substack, low reliability) complain about public scrutiny of spending [search summary]. Treat as unverified.

## Wellcome Leap

### Key outputs
- 12 programs across 30 countries and 160+ institutions as of 2025 [30]. No outcome data retrieved.

### Operating model
- Launched May 2020 with about $300M from the Wellcome Trust; Regina Dugan (ex-DARPA director) as CEO; $335M added in 2022 [30]. Teams are selected and funded against predefined targets and milestones, contrasted with Wellcome Trust grantmaking [31]; programs run 3-year milestone cycles per Wikipedia, with 5-10 year ambitions [30]. Wikipedia page carries a caution notice [30].

### What worked / Failure modes
- Too new for outcome evidence.

## Cross-org patterns

1. **Patient, concentrated money from a single funder.** Janelia (HHMI) [3], Allen [13], Broad [15][16], Arc [24] and Wellcome Leap [30] all start from a large founding commitment, often with fixed time horizons.
2. **Time-boxing as discipline.** Broad's 10-year gift [15], Janelia's 15-year model [3], FRO two-year go/no-go [21], Arc's 8-year terms [24] and Leap's milestone cycles [31] all build in review points. **Inference:** the structure, not the length, is the common feature.
3. **Shared infrastructure and open data** recur (hemibrain [7], Allen atlas [14], Arc atlas [26], Lean [20]).
4. **Two funding logics.** Fraunhofer disciplines through market revenue [10]; the philanthropic institutes discipline through review and milestones. Corporate labs (Microsoft) depend on parent strategy [18][19].
5. **Person-based vs problem-based.** HHMI/Arc invest in people [1][24]; FROs/ARIA/Leap invest in specified problems or programs [21][31][33].

## Lessons for modern operating models

1. **Fund people with long horizons and tolerance for failure when the goal is exploratory discovery.** [Evidence: moderate] The HHMI-vs-NIH comparison finds higher high-impact output, but it is observational with matching, and concerns investigators, not campuses [1].
2. **Build an external reality test into applied institutes by capping base funding.** [Evidence: moderate] Fraunhofer's roughly one-third cap and success-linked base funding are documented [10][11], but impact numbers are self-commissioned and contested [12].
3. **Pair open shared infrastructure with a bounded mission.** [Evidence: moderate] Hemibrain and Allen atlas are documented deliverables [7][14]; comparative cost-effectiveness is not.
4. **Use milestone gates and fixed lifespans for bottleneck projects.** [Evidence: weak] The FRO two-year go/no-go and Leap milestones exist [21][31], but no outcome evidence yet.
5. **Convert a time-limited gift to an endowment only after demonstrated performance.** [Evidence: weak] Broad's trajectory fits [15][16]; it is one case.
6. **Protect corporate labs' mandate explicitly or expect cyclical retrenchment.** [Evidence: inference] Based on the MSR closure and the CACM/blog analyses [18][19].
7. **Model replication depends on context (Fraunhofer needs a dense SME base and a state willing to restructure).** [Evidence: weak] Single policy commentary [search summary].

## Open questions / contested claims

- **FROs, ARIA, Arc and Leap are too new for outcome evidence.** Reported results are self-reported [22].
- **Budget and headcount conflicts:** Janelia ($80M/$130M/~$300M), Fraunhofer (EUR 3.4B vs 3.6B), Allen (825 vs 1,400).
- **Does Azoulay et al. support the Janelia model?** The paper studies HHMI investigators [1]; extrapolation is inference.
- **Fraunhofer's economic impact** is based on commissioned studies [12].
- **The Marblestone et al. Nature article** was not accessed; its specific claims (e.g. cost ranges, lifespan) are not reproduced here.
- **ARIA leadership and budget** details need primary verification [27].
