## Janelia Research Campus (HHMI)

### Key outputs
- The FlyEM team and Google released the hemibrain connectome in January 2020: roughly half a fly brain, about 25,000 neurons and more than 20 million connections, described as the largest synapse-resolution map of brain connectivity; Google began working with Janelia in 2014 [7].
- Wikipedia also lists the GCaMP calcium sensors and a 2018 whole-fly-brain image at neuronal resolution (secondary source) [5]. A Nature comment credits the GENIE project (calcium indicators) to a Janelia team and notes Janelia separates professionally staffed, large-scale projects from small academic labs [29].
- The Azoulay et al. work evaluates HHMI's *investigator* program, not Janelia itself (see below). Do not attribute those results to Janelia's campus model. **Inference.**

### Operating model
- Janelia opened in Ashburn, Virginia, in October 2006 as HHMI's first stand-alone research facility. It was modeled on AT&T Bell Labs and the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology [4][5]. Gerald Rubin was first executive director; Ronald Vale succeeded him in 2020 and Nelson Spruston in 2024 [5].
- Funding is institutional, so scientists do not write grants. In 2007 Rubin described the goal as "a million dollars a year" per group leader rather than a salary for life, with five-year renewable appointments and groups capped at six people [4]; Wikipedia now says six-year contracts [5].
- The "15-year research model" is confirmed by the eLife retrospective: research areas get fixed 15-year terms, with a new area starting about every five years [2]. HHMI's current Janelia page does not mention it and instead describes a pivot to "AI-in-the-Loop" with three initiatives [6]. Whether the 15-year design is still operative is **unverified**.
- The HHMI investigator program funds people rather than projects. Azoulay, Graff Zivin and Manso compared 1993-95 HHMI appointees with early-career prize winners (Pew, Searle, Beckman and similar) using propensity-score weighting and difference-in-differences [1]. The final sample was 417 scientists (69 HHMI, 348 controls). Preferred (SDD) estimates imply HHMI appointment raises publication rate by 39%, top-5%-cited papers by 55% and top-1%-cited papers by 97%; the naive cross-sectional gap is larger, and propensity weighting cut effects by about a third [1]. The earlier version of this profile cited "twice as many top-5% papers", "three times as many top-1%" and "50% more top-1% than MERIT grantees" from a secondary summary. None of those figures appear in the paper and they have been removed [1].

### What worked
- Shared, open, team-built infrastructure (connectomics, tools) that single academic labs struggle to fund [7].
- Long horizons plus person-based selection are associated with more high-impact output in the HHMI comparison, with the effect concentrated in the upper tail [1]. This is evidence about the investigator program; the Janelia campus model is a different, though related, design.

### Failure modes / unknowns
- Budget figures conflict and none is audited: $80M (2007) [4], $130M (c. 2019 flyer) [3], $300M (unsourced Wikipedia infobox) [5]. A third-party listing of HHMI's 2020 Form 990 shows about $1.08B in expenses; 15% of that is about $160M, closer to the flyer than to $300M (**Inference**; the 990 figure was not opened). No audited Janelia figure was retrieved.
- Selection effects: HHMI picks promising scientists. The authors address this with matching and DiD design, but the sample is small, the control group is prize winners rather than typical NIH grantees, and the authors note the two groups may be on different pre-appointment trends [1]. Residual confounding cannot be ruled out. **Inference.**
- I found no controlled evaluation of Janelia's 15-year, project-team model against alternatives. **Unknown.**

## Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft

### Key outputs
- 74 institutes and independent research units across Germany and more than 30,000 employees (profile page) [9]; 30,898 employees at end-2025, down 3.1% year on year [10]. An older page gave 76 institutes and nearly 32,000 staff (2024) [13]; the discrepancy is a date difference and is not explained by the sources. (Audio/video compression and other widely known Fraunhofer technologies were not verified and are therefore omitted.)

### Operating model
- Founded as Fraunhofer-Gesellschaft zur Förderung der angewandten Forschung e.V. in Munich on 26 March 1949, with Bavarian state secretary Hugo Geiger as patron and nuclear physicist Walther Gerlach as first president. It began as a small funding-distribution office; its first own institute (applied microscopy, Mannheim, seven staff) was founded on 1 June 1954 [11][12]. This resolves the earlier open question on founding year.
- Fraunhofer's own site states: "No more than one-third of Fraunhofer's budget comes from institutional funding," with at least two-thirds from competitive private-sector and public-sector contracts; the page also says base funding is for pre-competitive research [8]. The profile page frames the cap relative to the contract research budget [9]. Wikipedia reports base funding split 9:1 between federal and state governments [12].
- Three-way split, 2025 [8]: industry revenue EUR 966M (EUR 768M contracts plus EUR 198M licence fees, 31.0% of the operating budget); federal and state project funding EUR 1,254M (30.1%); base funding about 29-30% (the page gives the share, not a euro figure). Externally earned revenue was 71.1% of the operating budget [8].
- Total business volume was EUR 3.6B in 2025, "at the previous year's level" (so about EUR 3.6B in 2024), of which contract research was 88% (EUR 3.2B) [8]. The earlier EUR 3.4B figure from research-in-germany.org [13] is superseded. The remaining 12% is not itemized on the finances page, so the earlier claim that it was "federal/state funding" is withdrawn.
- The earlier claims that base funding is "linked to success, the key indicator being economic return" and that weak institutes shrink come from a CASRAI summary [15]; Fraunhofer's finances page does not say this, only that contract-winning pressure "fosters entrepreneurial thinking" [8]. Treat the success-linked-base-funding mechanism as **unverified**. The 83% contract-research share for 2019 was not re-checked.

### What worked
- A built-in market test: revenue measures demand for applied research and disciplines institutes [8][15].
- Commentary attributes the model's success partly to Germany's thousands of medium-sized engineering firms as customers [search summary of policy analysis, see 16].

### Failure modes / unknowns
- Impact figures come from Fraunhofer-commissioned research, so they are contested [16 and related commentary; not opened].
- NCB argues revenue shows demand but not knowledge transfer, and that the model may route around university-industry collaboration [16; not opened].
- A commentary argues that public-project-heavy contract work gives cost advantages over private competitors [search summary, see 15/16].
- Headcount fell 3.1% in 2025 while volume was flat [8][10]; the cause is not documented in the sources.
- Replicability depends on a state willing to close or restructure weak institutes and on a dense SME base [search summary]. **Inference:** transplanting the model without those conditions is risky.

## Allen Institute

### Key outputs
- Began with the Allen Brain Atlas; open data was a founding choice, as Allen's team initially considered charging for database access before opting for free access [18].

### Operating model
- Paul Allen announced $100M in 2003; the institute started with four employees, three-year contracts, a small rented lab and one big project [17][18]. Later funding mixes government, foundation and private sources [17].

### What worked
- A single, bounded, shared-resource mission (atlas) with open release [18].

### Failure modes / unknowns
- Headcount sources disagree (about 825 vs 1,400). No current budget or independent outcome evaluation retrieved.

## Broad Institute

### Key outputs
- Not independently documented in the sources I accessed. Omitted rather than asserted.

### Operating model
- Launched in May 2004 after planning among the Broads, MIT, Whitehead, Harvard and affiliated hospitals; the founding gift was $100M over 10 years, doubled to $200M, then a $400M endowment in 2008 to make it permanent, totaling $600M from the Broads [19][20]. Stanley Medical Research Institute gave $100M in 2007 [20]. More than 1,200 scientists and staff are affiliated from Harvard and MIT [20].

### What worked
- A time-limited gift that proved itself and was then converted to an endowment is a clear funder pattern [19][20]. Whether that proves the institute's scientific effectiveness is a separate question. **Inference.**

### Failure modes / unknowns
- No operating budget or outcome analysis retrieved. The founding year is stated variously as 2003 and 2004 [19].

## Microsoft Research

### Key outputs
- Founded in 1991 by Rick Rashid, who led it to 2013, growing to more than 850 researchers across nearly a dozen labs, doing both basic and applied research [21].

### Operating model
- Corporate-funded lab with open publication culture and academic ties [21].

### What worked
- Longevity and scale across 30+ years [21]. Specific discoveries and award counts were not verified here.

### Failure modes / unknowns
- The 2014 closure of the Silicon Valley lab, part of 18,000 job cuts, drew an open letter from researchers originally signed by 28; the research chief reaffirmed fundamental research commitment [22]. Reported job losses vary (about 50 vs "dozens") [22][23].
- CACM places this in a pattern with Bell Labs, PARC and DEC labs; a blog argues Bell Labs' freedom depended on its parent's monopoly cash [22][23]. That is interpretation, not measured fact. **Inference:** corporate labs' autonomy tracks parent profitability and strategy.

## Convergent Research and Focused Research Organizations (FROs)

### Key outputs
- Convergent's portfolio page lists 12 FROs: CHI-FRO (AI/mind models), Cultivarium (microbes), CWorthy (ocean carbon tools), Dragonfly (astrophysics), E11 Bio (brain circuit mapping), Echo Labs (ecosystems), EvE Bio (pharmome mapping), Forest Neurotech (ultrasound neurotech), Imprint (immune memory), Lean FRO (formal mathematics), Meridial (circuit maps) and Parallel Squared Technology Institute [24]. The page gives no launch dates, funders or status. Wikipedia lists a different, shorter roster (8 entries, text says 7 as of 2023) and uses "Worthy" and "ParallelSq" [27]. Bind Research appears in the 2025 retrospective [30] but not on the portfolio page [24].
- Forest Neurotech is now named Arbor Neuroscience (Convergent's own listing and Wikipedia [27]); the rename date and reason are not documented. It remains a nonprofit under Convergent, not a spin-out. The $14M Eric Schmidt commitment of March 2024 is from a search listing of a press release and was not re-opened.
- Lean FRO's own site lists sponsors including the Sloan Foundation, Simons Foundation, Richard Merkin, Alex Gerko (XTX Markets), Convergent, Harmonic and Amazon Automated Reasoning, and a year-4 roadmap; no launch date or milestone results are given [31].
- E11 Bio's PRISM post reports an 8x improvement in tracing accuracy (error-free run length) for genetically labelled neurons in a single mouse hippocampus (CA2/3) pilot of about 10 million cubic microns. "A clear path to connectomics at 100x lower cost" is a projection with no cost model, and the post says whole-mouse-brain scale is roughly 500 times larger and not yet demonstrated [26]. These are the organization's own claims, not independent validation.

### Operating model
- Nonprofit "start-ups" for bottleneck problems, incubated by Convergent (founded 2021, funded by Eric and Wendy Schmidt and Ken Griffin per Wikipedia) [27][28]. Adam Marblestone is chief executive [29].
- The founding Nature comment (Marblestone, Gamick, Kalil, Martin, Cvitkovic, Rodriques; Nature 601, 13 Jan 2022) proposed budgets of roughly $20M-$100M over about five years, cites Fraunhofer and Janelia among its precedents, and frames FROs as distinct from ARPA-style programmes [29]. The 2025 retrospective reports typical practice as 10-30 full-time staff, $20-50M and three to seven years, i.e. smaller and with a wider time range than proposed [30].
- Milestones: the 2025 authors recommend impact targets, go/no-go points that can justify preemptive abandonment, and intermediate milestones; they moved away from rigid DARPA-style milestones to a "North Star" with adaptive near-term goals [30]. A fixed two-year go/no-go appears only in a Forbes report [25], which was not opened, and is not in Convergent's about page or the 2025 article. It is **unverified** and the earlier statement that all FROs have one is withdrawn.
- Reported lessons from Convergent: mid-project funding top-ups are hard so most budget should be secured upfront; standard start-up metrics fit poorly; scientists often lack management training; high-level roles are hard to fill against big-tech pay; partnerships (e.g. Forest with Butterfly Network, CWorthy with CarbonPlan) keep teams lean [30]. These are the operator's own reflections.
- The authors report "the first wave of FRO transitions" but name no FRO that has completed, spun out or been wound down [30]. Nothing reviewed shows any FRO failing a gate. An ARIA residency in the UK reviewed nearly 100 applications and placed five founding teams in a six-month residency, with one UK FRO expected [30].

### What worked
- Early technical milestones reported by organizations themselves (e.g. [26]), and Convergent's claim that the feared talent shortage did not materialize [30]. No independent outcome data.

### Failure modes / unknowns
- Too new for outcome evidence. Dependence on a small donor base and the question of what happens after the fixed term are open (the 2025 article names transitions as an emerging issue) [30]. **Inference.**
- Status of individual FROs (active, transitioned, ended) could not be established from any page opened.

## Arc Institute

### Key outputs
- Arc Virtual Cell Atlas, launched in 2025 starting from over 300 million cells, with Tahoe-100M among datasets [34]; Evo genomic foundation models (described in secondary reports) [34].

### Operating model
- Nonprofit launched December 2021 with pledges above $650M, co-founded by Silvana Konermann, Patrick Hsu and Patrick Collison, partnered with Stanford, UC Berkeley and UCSF; core investigators get fully funded, renewable 8-year appointments, and affiliates remain at universities with partial Arc funding [32][33].

### What worked
- Fast visible outputs in computational biology [34]. Quality assessment is not available.

### Failure modes / unknowns
- Under four years old; no outcome evidence. Dependence on a few wealthy donors. **Inference.**

### Key outputs
- Second cohort of programme directors and new program areas announced April 2025 [40]. No outcome data retrieved.

### Operating model
- Backed with GBP 800M through 2025-26 [36][37], established January 2023; Ilan Gur, founder of Activate and ex-ARPA-E programme director, was named first CEO in 2022 after the first selection withdrew [37]. Wikipedia's infobox lists Kathleen Fisher as current CEO and a FY2024/25 budget of GBP 276M [35]; I could not confirm the transition details or dates.

### What worked
- Too early to say.

### Failure modes / unknowns
- Leadership turnover at the start [37]; critics (an opinion Substack, low reliability) complain about public scrutiny of spending [search summary]. Treat as unverified.

## Wellcome Leap

### Key outputs
- 12 programs across 30 countries and 160+ institutions as of 2025 [38]. No outcome data retrieved.

### Operating model
- Launched May 2020 with about $300M from the Wellcome Trust; Regina Dugan (ex-DARPA director) as CEO; $335M added in 2022 [38]. Teams are selected and funded against predefined targets and milestones, contrasted with Wellcome Trust grantmaking [39]; programs run 3-year milestone cycles per Wikipedia, with 5-10 year ambitions [38]. Wikipedia page carries a caution notice [38].

### What worked / Failure modes
- Too new for outcome evidence.

## Cross-org patterns

1. **Patient, concentrated money from a single funder.** Janelia (HHMI) [5], Allen [17], Broad [19][20], Arc [32] and Wellcome Leap [38] all start from a large founding commitment, often with fixed time horizons.
2. **Time-boxing as discipline.** Broad's 10-year gift [19], Janelia's 15-year research areas [2], Arc's 8-year terms [32], Leap's milestone cycles [39] and FRO fixed lifespans of roughly 3-7 years with go/no-go points [30] all build in review points. **Inference:** the structure, not the length, is the common feature. A specific two-year FRO gate is unverified (see FRO section).
3. **Shared infrastructure and open data** recur (hemibrain [7], Allen atlas [18], Arc atlas [34], Lean [31]).
4. **Two funding logics.** Fraunhofer disciplines through market revenue and a base-funding cap [8]; the philanthropic institutes discipline through review and milestones. Corporate labs (Microsoft) depend on parent strategy [22][23].
5. **Person-based vs problem-based.** HHMI/Arc invest in people [1][32]; FROs/ARIA/Leap invest in specified problems or programs [30][39][40].

## Lessons for modern operating models

1. **Fund people with long horizons and tolerance for failure when the goal is exploratory discovery.** [Evidence: moderate] The HHMI-vs-NIH comparison finds higher high-impact output (about +55% top-5% and +97% top-1% papers in the preferred estimates), concentrated in the upper tail. It is observational, rests on 69 HHMI appointees, uses early-career prize winners as controls, and concerns investigators, not campuses [1].
2. **Build an external reality test into applied institutes by capping base funding.** [Evidence: moderate] Fraunhofer's cap of one-third institutional funding, with at least two-thirds won competitively, is confirmed on its own site [8]. The mechanism that ties base funding to past success is unverified [15], and impact numbers are self-commissioned and contested [16].
3. **Pair open shared infrastructure with a bounded mission.** [Evidence: moderate] Hemibrain and Allen atlas are documented deliverables [7][18]; comparative cost-effectiveness is not.
4. **Use milestone gates and fixed lifespans for bottleneck projects.** [Evidence: weak] FRO milestone and go/no-go practice is documented by the operator [30], and Leap milestones exist [39], but no outcome evidence yet, and the specific two-year gate is unverified.
5. **Convert a time-limited gift to an endowment only after demonstrated performance.** [Evidence: weak] Broad's trajectory fits [19][20]; it is one case.
6. **Protect corporate labs' mandate explicitly or expect cyclical retrenchment.** [Evidence: inference] Based on the MSR closure and the CACM/blog analyses [22][23].
7. **Model replication depends on context (Fraunhofer needs a dense SME base and a state willing to restructure).** [Evidence: weak] Single policy commentary [search summary].

## Open questions / contested claims

- **FROs, ARIA, Arc and Leap are too new for outcome evidence.** Reported results are self-reported [26][30].
- **Budget and headcount conflicts:** Janelia ($80M 2007 / $130M c. 2019 / unsourced $300M; 600 vs 650 staff), Allen (825 vs 1,400). Fraunhofer's EUR 3.4B vs 3.6B is resolved to EUR 3.6B for 2024 and 2025 [8]; 74 vs 76 institutes reflects different dates [9][13].
- **Does Azoulay et al. support the Janelia model?** The paper studies HHMI investigators [1]; extrapolation is inference.
- **Is Janelia's 15-year research-area model still in force?** [2] confirms it as of 2019; [6] does not mention it.
- **Fraunhofer's economic impact** is based on commissioned studies [16].
- **FRO status and the two-year go/no-go:** no roster status or launch dates retrieved; the 2025 article reports transitions without naming them [30].
- **ARIA leadership and budget** details need primary verification [35].
