## 1. Edison / Menlo Park

### Key outputs
- Phonograph (1877) and improvements to Bell's telephone (carbon transmitter) [12].
- The first practical incandescent lamp, demonstrated 31 Dec 1879 [13].
- Menlo Park is widely described as the first industrial R&D lab, nicknamed the "invention factory" [11][12].

### Operating model
- Edison promised "a minor invention every ten days and a big thing every six months or so" [11].
- Small multidisciplinary core team: Charles Batchelor as experimental assistant, John Kruesi as machine-shop head, Francis Upton as theoretical assistant [13][12].
- Funding came first from Western Union, then from Edison Speaking Phonograph. A group of Western Union-linked investors including J. P. Morgan backed the light project. One source puts that funding at about $130,000 over roughly two and a half years [13].
- The lab ran as a business as well as a laboratory [13].

### What worked
- Integrated machine shop, chemistry and instrument capacity under one roof, with capital from outside investors [11][13].
- Targeting a commercial system (lamp) rather than a single device. **[Inference]** from [13].

### Failure modes
- It was the personal enterprise of one inventor, which made it hard to sustain. Menlo Park was abandoned, and sources differ on when: 1882 (followed by temporary sites until 1887) or 1887 with the move to West Orange [14].
- Most buildings were destroyed or moved [12]. **[Inference]** The model depended on Edison and on external financiers and did not become an institution. The GE and DuPont labs below did.

## 2. GE Research Laboratory (Whitney / Coolidge)

### Key outputs
- Ductile tungsten filaments. Coolidge joined in 1905. Britannica says he perfected the process by 1908, while the Lemelson Center says 1910. GE lamps used the filaments from 1911, and the patent issued in December 1913 (sources differ on dates). A 1928 US court held the 1913 patent not valid as an invention [18].
- Coolidge hot-cathode X-ray tube, 1913 to 1916. It built on Langmuir's high-vacuum electron-discharge work, and its basic design has never been replaced [18].
- Irving Langmuir (at GE 1909 to 1950) won the 1932 Nobel Prize in Chemistry [20]. Ivar Giaever (joined 1958) shared the 1973 Physics Prize for tunneling, with GE Schenectady as his affiliation [19]. GE says these are its only two Nobel laureates [20].

### Operating model
- Opened 15 Dec 1900 as an early (often called the first) US industrial research lab [15].
- Willis Whitney was first director (1900 to 1932) [17]. Coolidge became director in 1932 and retired in 1945 [18].
- Founding credit is disputed. Wikipedia and Clio credit Edison, Whitney and Steinmetz. The Edison Tech Center says GE president E. W. Rice recruited Whitney after Steinmetz declined to run the lab, and that Steinmetz first promoted the idea. NPS says Whitney got an offer from Rice [16][17].

### What worked
- Basic research tied to the company's lamp business. For example, Langmuir's vacuum work fed Coolidge's tube [18]. **[Inference]** that this coupling (science in service of a dominant product line) is the mechanism, not the labs' freedom alone.
- Two Nobel Prizes from one corporate lab [19][20].

### Failure modes
- Globalization of R&D under Immelt: Bangalore, Shanghai (2002) and Munich (2004), plus a $100 million Niskayuna renovation [21][22]. A source reports that Shanghai, Munich and Rio were closed in 2017, leaving Niskayuna and Bangalore [21].
- I found no source on GE Research's size or budget at its peak, so scale claims are not supported here.
- The Arora et al. finding of withdrawal from science by large US corporations [1] covers firms like this, but the paper's firm list was not available to me. **[Inference]** only.

## 3. DuPont Central Research (Carothers / nylon)

### Key outputs
- Nylon 66, first synthesized 28 Feb 1935 at the Experimental Station [9]. Hagley dates Carothers's first polyamide fiber to 1934 (sources differ) [8]. DuPont announced it in 1938, and stockings were shown at the 1939 World's Fair [7][5].
- Carothers' group also produced neoprene (a 1932 photograph of Carothers with neoprene is in the Hagley collection) [7]. **[Inference]** from the photo caption only.
- Carothers died by suicide in April 1937 (28 or 29 April, sources differ), about three weeks after the basic nylon patent application [7][8].

### Operating model
- The Experimental Station was founded in 1903 and first focused on explosives. After about a decade it became a vehicle for diversification [4].
- In 1927 Charles Stine argued for work aimed at discovering new scientific facts. The executive committee approved a modified version in March 1927, with a monthly budget and a mandate to hire about 25 chemists. The new lab was nicknamed "Purity Hall" [5].
- Carothers was recruited to lead polymer research with initial freedom for pure research. His start date is 1927 (job offer) or early 1928 (joined) depending on the source [6][7].
- Hounshell & Smith (Cambridge University Press, 1988) is the standard history. It was commissioned by Edward Jefferson, and the authors had company records and 61 oral-history interviews [3].

### What worked
- A fundamental-research bet paying off in a commercial system. The basic discovery came out of a polymer-theory program, followed by DuPont's scale-up to mass production [5][9]. **[Inference]** I could not confirm the nylon development cost figure, so none is given.

### Failure modes
- Corfam: a synthetic leather launched in the 1960s and withdrawn in 1972. The Hagley finding aid cites high processing cost and consumer rejection (comfort, porosity). Kanigel's paper argues it met many technical expectations but failed on human needs for comfort, feel and variety [10].
- Not everything DuPont is credited with came from Central Research. A newspaper letter says Kevlar, Nomex, Lycra and others came from business units, with nylon the Central Research success [10]. This is a weak source.
- The department was renamed in 1958 and merged with Development in 1975 [4]. **[Inference]** This is consistent with a shift from fundamental toward business-aligned research, but I did not find the Hounshell & Smith text to confirm that reading.

## 4. IBM Research (incl. Zurich, Almaden, Nobel Prizes, STM, relational DB)

### Key outputs
- Origins: Watson Scientific Computing Laboratory at Columbia, 1945, described as the first corporate pure-science facility in the US. Thomas J. Watson Research Center opened in 1961 [25].
- San Jose lab (1952): the IBM 350 disk file, first commercial moving-head hard disk, in the 305 RAMAC (available 4 Sept 1956, 5 MB on 50 24-inch disks) [28]. Moved to Almaden in 1986 [28].
- Relational model: Codd published in 1970 at San Jose [29]. System R (from 1973 or 1974, sources differ) was the first SQL implementation, designed by Chamberlin and Boyce, with Jim Gray contributing [29][30]. Chamberlin credits Codd with the key insights and calls the System R team "the carpenters" [30]. Codd received the 1981 Turing Award [29].
- Zurich lab (1956, moved to Rüschlikon 1962 to 1963; Ambros Speiser director 1956 to 1966; IBM's only research centre outside the US at the time) [27].
- STM: Binnig and Rohrer, Zurich, 1981 invention, 1986 Nobel Prize in Physics (quarter share each) [22][23].
- High-temperature superconductivity: Bednorz and Müller, Zurich, 1986 La-Ba-Cu-O oxide with Tc about 35 K, 1987 Nobel Prize, reportedly the shortest discovery-to-award gap for a scientific Nobel [24].
- Count of IBM Nobel laureates: AIP says five [26]; a Wikipedia mirror says six, with six Turing Awards (sources differ). Esaki (1973, Yorktown) is the fifth in the five-count, but I found only a weak source for his IBM affiliation [26]. Giaever is a GE laureate, not IBM [19].

### Operating model
- A multi-site network: New York, San Jose/Almaden and Zurich, with Zurich the first non-US site [27][28].
- Basic science and product-adjacent work sit in one organization. IBM Research made the disk drive and the relational model, which fed IBM products (SQL/DS 1981, renamed DB2 in 1983) [28][29].

### What worked
- Open-ended solid-state physics in Zurich yielded two Nobel Prizes within two years [22][24].
- Both STM and the superconductor are instruments or materials discoveries by small teams. **[Inference]** The sources give no team sizes.

### Failure modes
- Slow commercialization of relational databases. Britannica says IBM was slow to market the relational system. A company blog attributes this to protecting the IMS hierarchical product, which is interpretation and not primary evidence. Oracle reportedly shipped a relational product in 1979, before SQL/DS (1981) [29][31]. Sources are non-authoritative on motive, and one claims Codd was kept out of System R, which I could not corroborate [31].
- I found no authoritative source on IBM Research's downsizing or restructuring. Statements on decline here are **[Inference]** only.

## Cross-lab patterns

1. **Four institutional forms.** Edison's lab was personal and investor-financed [13]. GE's and IBM's were corporate labs created by firms with strong product bases. DuPont's was corporate but began with explosives and diversification [4]. Mowery's description of the corporate lab as bringing invention inside the firm and reducing the independent inventor's role is consistent with all four [32][33].
2. **Recurring Nobel-class science followed a coupling to a product line.** Langmuir to Coolidge to the lamp and X-ray business [18]; Carothers to nylon [5][9]; Zurich physics to IBM's electronics business [22][24]. This is an inference from the cases, not a finding in the sources.
3. **A recurrent gap between invention and commercialization.** Examples: Corfam [10], IBM's relational database [29], and Edison's inability to keep Menlo Park running [14].
4. **Later retrenchment or reorganisation.** GE closed overseas sites [21], DuPont renamed and merged its research unit [4], and Arora et al. document a decline in publications by US corporate scientists over 1980 to 2006 while the patent-related value of technical knowledge stayed stable [1][2].
5. **Founder-scientist leadership.** Whitney, Coolidge, Stine, Speiser and Edison each combined research and business advocacy [5][17][18][27]. **[Inference]**

## Lessons for modern operating models

1. **Tie exploratory science to a product platform the firm already owns.** Langmuir's vacuum work fed the lamp and X-ray businesses, and Carothers' polymer theory fed nylon. [Evidence: moderate] [5][9][18]
2. **Protect a small fundamental-research budget with explicit executive sponsorship.** Stine had to win committee approval and a hiring mandate for "pure" research. [Evidence: moderate] [5]
3. **Plan the hand-off from lab to business unit before the discovery.** Corfam, IBM's relational database and Menlo Park show different breaks in the chain. [Evidence: moderate for Corfam [10] and IBM [29]; weak for causal generalisation] 
4. **Do not treat patents as a substitute for in-house science.** Arora et al. find that firms keep valuing the output but fund the capability less. [Evidence: strong for the publication decline [1][2]; the causal story is contested, see below]
5. **Build institutions that outlast the founder.** Menlo Park ended with the move to West Orange [14], whereas GE's lab continued after Whitney for decades [18]. [Evidence: weak, two cases] **[Inference]**
6. **Distribute research across sites for talent access but expect cost and coordination pressure.** Zurich's success [22][24] contrasts with GE's expansion and then closure of overseas labs [21]. [Evidence: weak]
7. **Treat instrument- and materials-led discovery as a portfolio bet.** STM and the oxide superconductor came from small groups. Judge on the portfolio, not individual projects. [Evidence: weak] **[Inference]**

## Open questions / contested claims

- **Who founded the GE lab?** Wikipedia and Clio credit Edison, Whitney and Steinmetz. The Edison Tech Center and NPS say Rice recruited Whitney and Steinmetz declined to run it [16][17].
- **When did Menlo Park close?** 1882 or 1887 [14].
- **Coolidge dates.** Ductile tungsten 1908 (Britannica) vs 1910 (Lemelson). Patent validity was struck down in 1928 [18].
- **Carothers start date and death date.** 1927 (offer) vs 1928 (joined), and 28 vs 29 April 1937 [6][7][8].
- **IBM Nobel count.** Five (AIP) vs six (Wikipedia mirror). Esaki's affiliation needs a primary check [26].
- **Why IBM was slow on relational.** Protecting IMS is plausible but rests on a blog. Claims about Codd's exclusion are uncorroborated [29][31].
- **The Arora et al. causal story.** The NBER version reportedly attributes the decline to globalization and narrower firm scope, while the published abstract I saw did not [1][2]. I could not verify this.
- **DuPont's post-1950s decline in fundamental research.** Not verified against Hounshell & Smith [3].
- **Not covered because sources were not retrievable:** nylon development cost, GE and IBM headcounts and budgets, Mowery & Rosenberg's chapter-level arguments, and IBM Research's restructurings.
