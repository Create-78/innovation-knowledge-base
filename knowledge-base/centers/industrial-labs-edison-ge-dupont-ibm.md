## 1. Edison / Menlo Park

### Key outputs
- Phonograph (1877) and the carbon telephone transmitter [17][16].
- The first practical incandescent lamp, shown publicly at Menlo Park in December 1879 [17]. Wikipedia describes the "Village of Light" visitors of February 1880 [17].
- "First industrial research laboratory" is how Wikipedia and ETHW describe it [17][16]. This is contested. Mowery says the in-house industrial lab first appeared in the German chemicals industry in the 1870s [37], and the Edison Tech Center says German pharmaceutical labs preceded GE's [21]. A defensible claim is "first to be run as an invention factory by an independent inventor" **[Inference]**.

### Operating model
- Edison promised "a minor invention every ten days and a big thing every six months or so". This quote was in the first draft and was not found in the Edison Papers essays I read **[unverified]**.
- The building cost about $2,500. Edison told Western Union's president that his machinery and apparatus cost about $40,000 [13].
- Small core team: Batchelor and Adams from the start [13]. Francis Upton joined later for the lighting work [17]. John Kruesi's role as machine-shop head was not re-checked **[unverified]**.
- Funding: Edison Papers name Western Union, then Edison Speaking Phonograph [14]. Wikipedia credits the quadruplex sale for the construction [17]. The earlier "$130,000 over two and a half years" figure and the J. P. Morgan syndicate came from PBS and the NPS handbook and are **[unverified]** [19].
- Edison "shut down public and reporter access" and managed his image to attract investment [17].

### What worked
- An integrated shop, chemistry and instrument capacity with outside capital, and growth from 5 staff to 25 in two years [13][14].
- Aiming at a commercial system (lamp plus distribution) rather than one device. **[Inference]**

### Failure modes
- **Closure date, resolved.** The sources measure different things. Edison Papers: he "all but deserted" Menlo Park within six years of 1876. He moved the lamp factory to Harrison in spring 1882 and made a formal break in September 1882 by renting laboratory space in lower Manhattan [15]. Wikipedia and NPS give 1887 as the move of home and lab to West Orange [17][18]. So 1882 is the end of Menlo Park as Edison's working lab and 1887 is the new lab. The 1887 date is not the closure date.
- The cause was business geography: manufacturing and customers sat in and around New York [15]. It was not a collapse of the lab.
- West Orange itself shrank. It had up to 200 men, and the company peaked at 11,000 staff in World War I. Within four years staff fell below 2,000, and only seven men still worked in the labs in 1930 [18].
- **[Inference]** The model depended on one founder and on his shifting business interests, even though a bigger successor lab was built.

## 2. GE Research Laboratory (Whitney / Coolidge)

### Key outputs
- Ductile tungsten filaments. Coolidge joined in 1905. Wikipedia gives 1908 for the ductile tungsten work [27], and the Lemelson Center's 1910 was not re-checked. GE lamps used the filaments from 1911, and the patent issued in 1913. A 1928 US court held the 1913 patent not valid as an invention [24]. Coolidge rejected an award in 1926 partly on that ground [24].
- Coolidge hot-cathode X-ray tube, invented 1913, patent granted 1916. Its basic design is still in use [24].
- Langmuir's gas-filled bulb (1916) is credited by Wikipedia with helping GE reach 96% of US incandescent lamp sales by 1928 (uncited there) [27].
- Nobel Prizes: Langmuir, Chemistry 1932, sole prize, GE Schenectady [25]. Giaever, Physics 1973, one-quarter share, GE Schenectady [25]. Giaever worked at GE while pursuing a PhD at RPI [26]; his 1958 start date was not re-checked.
- **"GE's only two Nobel laureates" needs a qualifier.** GE's 2002 release says GE "has had two Nobel prize winners" [28]. GE's 2012 article lists three more with GE ties: Feynman and Lawrence as research fellows and Perl as an engineer for two years [26].

### Operating model
- Opened in 1900 [21][22].
- Willis Whitney was recruited by E. W. Rice and Elihu Thomson. He turned the offer down several times and tried the job part-time from MIT, then left MIT for the full-time directorship in May 1904 [23]. He later named Coolidge as successor [23]. Coolidge was director from 1932 and retired in 1944 per Wikipedia. The earlier "1945" is **[unverified]** [24].
- **Founding credit, resolved in part.** Wikipedia's GE page credits Edison, Whitney and Steinmetz [27]. The Edison Tech Center says Steinmetz first promoted the idea in the late 1890s. Rice wanted Steinmetz to head the lab and Steinmetz refused. Thomson then also refused and suggested Whitney [21]. The NPS landmark text has Rice writing to recruit Whitney [22]. Edison's name reflects corporate lineage (GE's 1892 formation), not involvement in the lab [28]. **[Inference]**

### What worked
- Basic research tied to the company's lamp business: Langmuir's vacuum and gas work fed lamp and X-ray products [24][27]. **[Inference]** This coupling is the mechanism, not the lab's freedom alone.
- Two Nobel Prizes from one corporate lab [25].

### Failure modes
- **Globalization, corrected.** Bangalore opened in 2000 as GE's first R&D centre outside the US, before Immelt became CEO. The 2002 release announced Munich ($52 million, 150 scientists, operating early 2004) and a Shanghai centre due early 2003. It also announced a $100 million expansion in New York [28]. Wikipedia lists Shanghai 2000-2017, Munich 2003-2017 and Rio 2014-2017 [27]. In October 2017 the new CEO closed Shanghai, Munich and Rio, leaving Niskayuna and Bangalore. The stated aim was cost-cutting and moving the work into business units [27].
- No source on GE Research's budget at its peak. GE spent more than $5 billion on R&D in 2016 company-wide (Wall Street Journal via Wikipedia) [27].
- The Arora et al. finding of withdrawal from science by large US corporations [1] covers firms like this, but the paper's firm list was not available. **[Inference]** only.

## 3. DuPont Central Research (Carothers / nylon)

### Key outputs
- Nylon 66 was first synthesized on 28 Feb 1935 at the Experimental Station [11]. The work on polyamides began in 1934 [11]. DuPont announced nylon in 1938 and took the patent in September 1938 [11]. Stockings were shown at the 1939 World's Fair and sold from 1940, with 64 million pairs in the first year [11]. DuPont began the Seaford plant in 1938, sized at 12 million lb a year [6].
- Neoprene was synthesized in spring 1930 by the Carothers group [11].
- Carothers died on 29 April 1937 [8][11], which resolves the 28 vs 29 April question. His cause of death was not re-checked in the pages I read **[unverified]**. The earlier "three weeks after the basic patent application" is dropped. The pages I read give a September 1938 patent date [11]. Nylon was announced about 16 months after his death [11].
- The cost figure: an ACS resource, seen only as a search snippet, says that by 1938 more than 230 chemists and engineers had worked on nylon at a cost of $27 million. **[Unverified]**; it was not confirmed in Hounshell & Smith.

### Operating model
- The Eastern Laboratory (1902) and Experimental Station (1903) served explosives and dealt with outside inventors. DuPont diversified from 1908 with Fabrikoid and Arlington [3].
- After the 1921 decentralization, a small Chemical Department did central research for the autonomous business departments [3].
- In December 1926 Charles Stine's memo argued for "pure science work". The executive committee approved a modified version in March 1927. Stine got $25,000 a month, a mandate to hire 25 of the best chemists, and funds for a new laboratory soon called "Purity Hall" [6].
- Carothers was recruited in 1927 and started on 6 Feb 1928 per Wikipedia [11]. Hagley dates his time at the Station to 1928 [9]. Linda Hall says he took the job in 1928 [8]. The "offer 1927 / joined 1928" split is therefore explained. Linda Hall says DuPont offered a salary twice his Harvard pay [8].
- Hounshell & Smith (Cambridge, 1988) is the standard history. I could not read it. A TU Delft student summary says that in 1946 DuPont adopted fundamental research as its growth model, the "nylon model", because growth by acquisition was no longer politically feasible [3].

### What worked
- A fundamental-research bet paying off in a commercial system. The discovery came from a polymer-theory program, followed by DuPont's scale-up [6][11]. **[Inference]**
- DuPont's plan was an explicit organisational design: a central lab with a pure-science mandate and a named budget [6].

### Failure modes
- **Corfam, dates corrected.** Introduced in 1963 and halted eight years later (Kanigel) [12]. DuPont withdrew it in 1971 and sold the rights to a company in Poland (Wikipedia) [12b]. The earlier "1972" is not supported. Kanigel argues that it met many technical expectations but failed on comfort, feel and variety [12]. The Hagley finding aid's cost and porosity reasons are **[unverified]**.
- Per the TU Delft summary of Hounshell & Smith [3]: after World War II research spending grew but its productivity did not, "development fell through the cracks", and the 1961 New Ventures program failed. In the 1970s the research organizations were used to keep existing businesses healthy, and the Greenwalt life-science lab followed in 1980. This is weak, secondhand support for the retrenchment reading.
- Mowery (citing Hounshell & Smith) says DuPont obtained many major product and process innovations before 1940 from outside sources and developed them in-house [37]. Nylon and neoprene were the exceptions that came from the internal program. **[Inference]**
- A newspaper letter says Kevlar, Nomex, Lycra and others came from business units [12d]. This is a weak source and was not opened.
- Renamed Central Research Dept 1958 and merged with Development 1975 [5, unverified in this pass].

## 4. IBM Research (incl. Zurich, Almaden, Nobel Prizes, STM, relational DB)

### Key outputs
- Origins: Watson Scientific Computing Laboratory at Columbia, 1945, with a mandate to "explore science, forget profits". IBM calls it the first corporate pure-science research facility in the US [29]. The Thomas J. Watson Research Center opened in 1961 [31].
- San Jose lab (1952) [30][31]: the IBM 350 disk file in the 305 RAMAC, launched 1956 [31]. IBM's own page says RAMAC stored 5 million characters, about 5-10 MB [30]. The "50 24-inch disks" and "4 Sept 1956" details were not re-checked **[unverified]**. The "moved to Almaden in 1986" detail was not confirmed either; Wikipedia says the lab moved to a new Silicon Valley site in 2025 [31].
- Relational model: Codd's 1970 paper at San Jose **[unverified]**. System R began in 1974 and was the first SQL implementation. Its first customer was Pratt & Whitney in 1977 [33]. Wikipedia says System R was "led by Edgar Codd". Chamberlin says Codd had the key insights and System R's team were "the carpenters", with large contributions from Jim Gray [34]. The Wikipedia wording looks too strong. SQL authorship by Chamberlin and Boyce was not re-checked **[unverified]**.
- Zurich lab: opened in Adliswil in 1956, moved to Rüschlikon in 1962 [31]. Ambros Speiser was director 1956-1966, and it was "the only research center of IBM outside of the USA at the time" [32].
- STM: Binnig and Rohrer, Zurich, 1986 Physics Prize, one-quarter share each (Ruska took the other half) [25]. Both listed IBM Zurich as affiliation. The 1981 invention date was not re-checked.
- High-temperature superconductivity: Bednorz and Müller, Zurich, 1987 Physics Prize, half share each, affiliation IBM Zurich [25]. The "shortest gap to a Nobel" claim and Tc about 35 K are **[unverified]**.
- **Nobel count.** Nobel Foundation pages confirm five people with IBM affiliations at award: Esaki (1973, IBM Watson Research Center, Yorktown Heights, one-quarter share), Binnig, Rohrer, Bednorz and Müller [25]. This settles Esaki's affiliation. Wikipedia's lead says IBM employees "garnered six Nobel Prizes" and seven Turing Awards [31]. The sixth could not be identified, so the six-vs-five question is unresolved. Giaever is a GE laureate, not IBM [25].

### Operating model
- A multi-site network: Yorktown/Columbia, San Jose/Almaden and Zurich, with Zurich the first European lab [31][32].
- Basic science and product-adjacent work sit in one organization. IBM Research made the disk drive and the relational model, which fed IBM products [30][33]. SQL/DS 1981 and DB2 1983 are **[unverified]**.
- IBM's research arm was set up by company leadership. Watson Sr. had a decades-long academic partnership with Columbia that predates the lab [29].

### What worked
- Open-ended solid-state physics in Zurich yielded two Nobel Prizes in consecutive years [25].
- Both STM and the superconductor are instrument or materials discoveries by small teams. **[Inference]** The sources give no team sizes.

### Failure modes
- Slow commercialization of relational databases. The Register notes that System R had to "convince a skeptical world" [34]. Claims that IBM protected IMS, that Oracle shipped in 1979 before SQL/DS, and that Codd was kept out of System R rest on a blog and Britannica snippets that I did not open **[unverified]** [35][36].
- I found no authoritative source on IBM Research's downsizing. Wikipedia mentions about 80 researchers made redundant at one site in 2021 (site not identified in my read) [31]. Statements on decline are **[Inference]** only.

## Cross-lab patterns

1. **Four institutional forms.** Edison's lab was personal and investor-financed [13][14]. GE's and IBM's were corporate labs created by firms with strong product bases. DuPont's was corporate and began with explosives and diversification [3]. Mowery describes the corporate lab as bringing development inside the firm and reducing the importance of the independent inventor as a source of patents [37]. He also says that before 1940 these labs often monitored and acquired outside technology rather than only creating new technology [37].
2. **Antitrust as a driver.** Mowery argues that US antitrust, which discouraged horizontal mergers, pushed firms toward R&D for diversification, citing DuPont's early diversification [37]. The earlier draft said nothing on this.
3. **Recurring Nobel-class science followed a coupling to a product line.** Langmuir to Coolidge to the lamp and X-ray business [24][27]; Carothers to nylon [6][11]; Zurich physics to IBM's electronics business [25]. This is an inference from the cases, not a finding in the sources.
4. **A recurrent gap between invention and commercialization.** Examples: Corfam [12], IBM's relational database [34], and Edison's lab leaving its original site in 1882 [15].
5. **Later retrenchment or reorganisation.** GE closed overseas sites in 2017 [27]. DuPont's 1961 New Ventures program failed and the research unit was renamed and merged [3]. Arora et al. document a decline in publications by large-firm scientists over 1980 to 2007, with the patent-related value of technical knowledge stable [1].
6. **Founder-scientist leadership.** Whitney, Coolidge, Stine, Speiser and Edison each combined research and business advocacy [6][23][24][32]. **[Inference]**

## Lessons for modern operating models

1. **Tie exploratory science to a product platform the firm already owns.** Langmuir's vacuum work fed the lamp and X-ray businesses, and Carothers' polymer theory fed nylon. [Evidence: moderate] [6][11][24][27] Caveat from this pass: Mowery shows that pre-1940 labs also served to monitor and absorb outside technology, and that DuPont got many of its major innovations from outside sources [37]. Nylon and neoprene are the exceptions, so the "own the platform" lesson holds for the big discoveries but is not the typical lab output.
2. **Protect a small fundamental-research budget with explicit executive sponsorship.** Stine had to win committee approval, a $25,000 monthly budget and a hiring mandate for "pure" research. [Evidence: moderate, now with figures from a primary-style history article] [6]
3. **Plan the hand-off from lab to business unit before the discovery.** Corfam (1963-1971), IBM's relational database and the Edison lab show different breaks in the chain. [Evidence: moderate for Corfam [12]; weak for IBM, since motive claims are unverified [34][35]; weak for causal generalisation]
4. **Do not treat patents as a substitute for in-house science.** Arora et al. find that firms keep valuing the output but fund the capability less. [Evidence: strong for the publication decline [1]; the NBER abstract associates it with globalization and narrower firm scope]
5. **Build institutions that outlast the founder.** Changed in this pass: Menlo Park was left within six years because Edison's manufacturing moved [15], not because the lab failed. Its successor lasted decades and then shrank to seven men by 1930 [18]. GE's lab continued after Whitney [24]. [Evidence: weak, three cases] **[Inference]**
6. **Distribute research across sites for talent access but expect cost and coordination pressure.** Zurich's success [25] contrasts with GE's expansion in 2000-2004 and the 2017 closures [27][28]. The 2017 closures came with a CEO change and a company-wide cost cut, so they are weak evidence on site distribution itself. [Evidence: weak]
7. **Treat instrument- and materials-led discovery as a portfolio bet.** STM and the oxide superconductor came from small groups. Judge on the portfolio, not individual projects. [Evidence: weak] **[Inference]**

## Open questions / contested claims

- **Who founded the GE lab?** Rice and Thomson recruited Whitney, and Steinmetz promoted the idea and declined to run it [21][22][23]. Wikipedia's inclusion of Edison reflects GE's corporate lineage [27][28]. Resolved to the extent the sources allow.
- **When did Menlo Park close?** Resolved: effectively 1882 (formal break September 1882), with the move to West Orange in 1887 [15][18].
- **Which lab was "first"?** Menlo Park and GE are each called first. Mowery and the Edison Tech Center point to earlier German chemical labs [21][37].
- **Coolidge dates.** Ductile tungsten 1908 (Wikipedia) vs 1910 (Lemelson, not re-checked). Retirement 1944 (Wikipedia) vs 1945 (earlier draft, unverified). The patent was struck down in 1928 [24].
- **Carothers.** Start date resolved (offer 1927, joined 6 Feb 1928) and death date resolved (29 April 1937). Cause of death and the patent timing are not verified.
- **IBM Nobel count.** Five people confirmed through Nobel Foundation pages. Wikipedia says six prizes and the sixth is unidentified [25][31].
- **Why IBM was slow on relational.** Still rests on blog and snippet sources. Codd's exclusion from System R is uncorroborated [34][36].
- **The Arora et al. causal story.** The NBER abstract does associate the decline with globalization and narrower firm scope, rather than publication practices [1]. The SMJ abstract was not opened [2].
- **DuPont's post-1950s decline in fundamental research.** Only a student summary of Hounshell & Smith was read [3].
- **Not covered because sources were not retrievable:** the $27 million nylon figure, GE and IBM headcounts and budgets over time, Mowery & Rosenberg's chapter-level arguments, and IBM Research's restructurings.
