## Pixar

### Key outputs
- Toy Story 2: Disney first planned a direct-to-video sequel (announced March 1997). After Disney executives saw story reels in November 1997 the plan became theatrical, which was announced to the crew on 5 February 1998. Pixar's leadership judged the existing story unworkable and Lasseter took over; with a fixed release date the film had to be finished in about nine months, adding about 12 minutes. A deletion error reportedly wiped about 90% of two years of work, recovered from a backup on an employee's home computer. The film cost about $90 million and grossed about $511 million [3]. The earlier draft's "nearly $500 million" was most likely box office, not cost. Wikipedia's account does not use the word "Braintrust"; the link between the rescue and the Braintrust comes from book summaries [6] (snippet only).
- Disney agreed on 24 January 2006 to buy Pixar for about $7.4 billion in stock, closing 5 May 2006. Lasseter became chief creative officer of Pixar and Walt Disney Animation Studios and Catmull president of both; in 2007 they also received Disneytoon Studios [2][11].
- Lasseter left Disney and Pixar at the end of 2018 after a leave over acknowledged "missteps" with employees; Pete Docter was named chief creative officer on 19 June 2018. Catmull announced his retirement on 23 October 2018 and advised through July 2019 [2][11]. The earlier draft omitted these leadership changes.

### Operating model
- Catmull's HBR article (September 2008) argues against over-valuing the initial idea relative to the people and process that develop it. The introduction states this and the "finding good ideas vs. good people" anecdote [1]. The three practices (creative authority with project leaders, peer sharing of work-in-progress, removing barriers between disciplines) come from the publisher abstract via search results; the full text was not readable.
- The Braintrust grew out of Lasseter's circle: Stanton, Docter, Unkrich and Ranft, later widened to others including Catmull, with fluid membership by film [4]. Catmull: "the Braintrust should have no authority"; the unwritten norm is candor without anyone feeling attacked [4]. Members have different ranks but are equal in the room, and bad ideas are not punished [5]. Wikipedia describes reviewers as directors, writers and lead storyboard artists reviewing one another's projects [2]. The director decides which notes to adopt (book summary [6], snippet only).
- "Notes Day" (a company-wide feedback day, planned from early 2013) comes from a book summary [6] and was not re-verified.

### What worked
- The design separates diagnosis from command: critique is pooled, decision stays with the director [4][6]. Edmondson frames this as institutionalised candor [7] (snippet only).
- *Inference:* Peer reviewers who are themselves directors have standing and a stake in the next review, which plausibly sustains reciprocity. No source tests this.

### Failure modes / criticisms
- Reported failed replication of Braintrusts for technical groups [6] remains a single secondary report.
- Post-Disney drift: one Substack analysis counts only 9 of 18 films after the first ten as original [8]; another report says Pixar now makes more sequels than originals [10] (both snippet only; not audited figures). Layoffs: 175 employees (about 14%) in May 2024; a July 2026 WARN notice listed 108 affected employees [2]. Trade reporting links the 2026 round to weak performance of recent films [9] (snippet only).
- No independent empirical study of Braintrust effectiveness was found. **Evidence gap.**

### Transferability
- *Inference:* The Braintrust transfers as a pattern (advice without veto, peers not hierarchy) where work-in-progress is visible and iterable. It transfers poorly where the "director" lacks real decision rights or trust is low.

## Amazon

### Key outputs
- Lab126 was founded in 2004 by Gregg Zehr, formerly of Palm; the first Kindle (US$399) was released 19 November 2007 after about three years of development [15]. A "sold out in under six hours" claim was not confirmed in any opened page.
- AWS: Wikipedia dates the first AWS offering (web-developer APIs) to 2002 and the first infrastructure service, SQS, to a November 2004 beta; S3 launched 14 March 2006 and EC2 followed in August 2006 (beta) [13][14]. Wikipedia's Amazon page gives EC2 as 2008, which probably reflects general availability; sources disagree on the base date. Amazon itself says it launched AWS "in the spring of 2006" after seeing how hard and costly infrastructure was to manage and how it distracted teams from innovating [12]. AWS operating profit was US$45.6 billion on US$128.7 billion revenue in 2025 [13].

### Operating model
- **Working Backwards / PR/FAQ:** Amazon describes it as a way to vet ideas by defining the customer experience first, with a press release of under a page and an FAQ of at most five pages [23]. Bryar and Carr say most major Amazon initiatives since 2004 used it and that it was devised through trial and error and "Jeff's insight" around 2005, when SWOT analysis, market sizing and pro-forma P&Ls failed to force deep thinking about customers [24]. The silent-reading "Narrative" meeting is attributed to secondary guides (snippet only).
- **Six-page memos:** Bezos's 2017 letter confirms the practice: no slide decks, narratively structured six-page memos read silently at the start of meetings like a "study hall"; authors' names do not appear because the memo is from the team [21]. The 2004 Bezos email origin is repeated in secondary sources and was not verified.
- **Two-pizza teams:** Not found in the 2016-2021 shareholder letters [20][21][22] (earlier letters were not retrievable). Wikipedia's Bezos page describes a "two-pizza rule" as a preference that meetings be small enough for two pizzas [26]. The team-size version rests on a 2004 eWeek article via secondary sources and is unverified.
- **Single-threaded teams / leaders:** Bryar and Carr trace the model to growth-stage problems: multiple business units and over 500 headquarters staff meant constant resource contention, the S-team spending time on allocation disputes, and business-unit leaders feeling powerless ("learned helplessness") [25]. Jassy's 2021 letter states the principle: organise builders into teams as separable and autonomous as possible, because teams cannot be deep on customers in several areas and mature businesses win resource contention [22]. This contradicts the earlier draft's reading that the origin was a finding that leader quality, not team size, predicted success; that reading came from reader notes and was not supported.

### What worked
- Kindle and AWS both became durable businesses [12][13][15]. *Inference:* the written-narrative plus single-owner combination forces clarity about the customer and accountability. No controlled comparison exists.
- Bezos's 2018 letter says the Fire Phone "was a failure" but that Amazon took its learnings "(as well as the developers)" to accelerate Echo and Alexa, whose development started around the same time, and that "no customer was asking for Echo" [20].

### Failure modes / criticisms
- Fire Phone: announced 18 June 2014, shipped on AT&T 25 July 2014 at $199/$299 on contract and $650 off contract; Amazon reported a $170 million write-down and over $83 million of unsold inventory in its October 2014 results; production ended in August 2015 [17]. Price cuts within weeks are reported by Variety [18] (snippet only).
- Former employees say Bezos "obsessively monitored" the product, that even small decisions went to him, and that Dynamic Perspective drove the design; he later called it a "bold bet" [17]. Fast Company/Design News coverage [19] was not opened.
- *Inference:* Process (customer-backwards documents, single owner) does not protect against a founder-driven product thesis, but no source says whether a PR/FAQ was written for the Fire Phone.

### Transferability
- *Inference:* The narrative memo and PR/FAQ are the most portable elements (cheap, text-only). Single-threaded structure presupposes service boundaries and a leader pool able to run an area; the authors' own account ties it to a scale problem, so small teams may not need it [25].

## 3M

### Key outputs
- Post-it Notes, from opened sources: Spencer Silver, trying to make a strong adhesive for aircraft, made a low-tack, reusable adhesive (acrylate copolymer microspheres) in 1968, patented 1972; he promoted it internally for years [31][33]. Art Fry's hymnal-bookmark idea is dated 1973 [29][34] or 1974 [30][32][33]; sources conflict and no 3M primary was reachable. Fry's note-to-supervisor test convinced him the product was a sticky note; management doubted its saleability, and Fry's logging of internal use helped change minds [34]. Test-marketing as "Press 'n Peel" in four cities began in 1977 with disappointing results; the 1978 "Boise Blitz" free-sample test renamed it Post-it, with roughly 90-95% of recipients saying they would buy (sources vary) [29][31]. National U.S. launch followed in 1980 (6 April per Wikipedia's 3M page); the team won 3M's Golden Step Award and the Outstanding New Product Award in 1981 [29][32]. Roughly three years to win management and three more to reach market is one account [34]. The canary-yellow colour was chosen by chance (scrap paper next door), and Fry's patent for the note as sold dates to 1993 [32]. Wikipedia notes Fry's co-creator credit is disputed by some [32].
- Platforms: BusinessWeek (2007) says Buckley concentrated funding in 45 "core" technology areas [28]; 3M pages cited 46 and later 49 platforms (snippet only; 3M pages returned 403).

### Operating model
- BusinessWeek states that "official company policy allowed employees to use 15% of their time to pursue independent projects" and that workers could seek funding from several company sources [28]. A 3M page said the practice dates to about 1948 (snippet only; could not open). Wikipedia confirms McKnight's 1948 management statement (including "Mistakes will be made") but does not mention a 15% rule [36 background]. The date, the exact wording of the rule, whether it requires no manager approval, and a sharing condition are unverified. The "McKnight chose 15% from coffee-break time" story is anecdote with no document. A study-note source placing the rule in the 1930s conflicts with the 1948 date and is low quality.
- Post-it and the 15% rule: The Chemical Engineer says Fry tested the idea at work "using 3M's policy" of 15% own-choice time [34]; Wikipedia says Fry used the sanctioned "permitted bootlegging" policy [32]; a University of Houston Engines piece quotes 3M asking technical people to spend 15 percent of their time "trying out new, even useless, ideas" and places Silver's experiments there [35]. All are secondary; none shows a 3M record. Treat "Post-it came from 15% time" as widely repeated but not primary-verified. The product also needed years of team, production and marketing work beyond any individual's spare time [34][29].
- Technology platforms are shared capabilities that divisions combine into products (3M description, snippet only). Fast Company links 15% time to the Post-it and to about 22,800 patents without isolating causation (snippet only) [37].

### What worked
- Post-it is the canonical case of a technology waiting years for a user problem [29][33]. *Inference:* internal seminars and a share-your-findings norm let a solution circulate until a need appears; Fry found Silver's adhesive by attending one of his talks [30][32].

### Failure modes / criticisms
- **Six Sigma under McNerney (verified against the BusinessWeek PDF, 11 June 2007 [28]).** McNerney, the first outsider CEO, cut 8,000 jobs (about 11%), imported Six Sigma (thousands of black belts, near-universal green-belt training), cut capital spending 22% in his first full year, and kept R&D spending roughly flat at just over $1 billion a year from 2001 to 2005. Operating margin rose from 17% (2001) to 23% (2005). The article says that 3M, which prided itself on drawing at least one-third of sales from products released in the previous five years, was at about one-quarter and calls this "not coincidental"; this is the journalist's claim, not a proven cause. Quoted critics: Eric von Hippel (innovation projects "took a backseat"), Fry (blames Six Sigma), and an R&D vice-president who said the process was "getting in the way of … actual invention". A Benner and Tushman patent study found that after quality drives, patents based on prior work took a larger share [28].
- Successor George Buckley called invention "by its very nature a disorderly process" and removed researchers' obligation to hew to Six Sigma objectives; the article says the R&D budget would grow 20% in 2007 to $1.5 billion [28]. The earlier draft's "R&D cut about 25% in the prior four years" and "up 11% in 2007" came from a Design News snippet that was not opened and conflicts with the BusinessWeek figures, so it is dropped.
- Montgomery (2008) discusses whether Six Sigma stifles innovation [42]. Causation remains contested.
- *Evidence gap:* no peer-reviewed measurement of 15% time's output effect was found; no data on actual uptake of the 15% policy was found.

### Transferability
- *Inference:* Slack-time policies transfer only with the surrounding system (technical ladder, internal sharing, platform reuse, tolerance for slow payback). The McNerney episode suggests that efficiency metrics built for repetitive operations can crowd out exploratory work, though causation is contested.

## Toyota

### Key outputs
- Prius: Toyota announced its Earth Charter in January 1992; R&D executive vice president Yoshiro Kimbara created the G21 committee in September 1993; the G21 team's first official meeting was 1 February 1994. Wikipedia names Takehisa Yaegashi as development lead and Takeshi Uchiyamada as given the task in 1994; a concept car with a hybrid system was shown 27 October 1995 and the production NHW10 went on sale in Japan on 10 December 1997, meeting a three-year target "barely" [49]. Wikipedia says engineers reviewed over 100 hybrid designs; the Fortune article [48] (80 alternatives, via snippet) could not be opened, so the count is unresolved. Treat lead-developer credit and the count as unresolved.

### Operating model
- Toyota's own page describes TPS as built on two pillars, jidoka ("automation with a human touch": stop on abnormality) and Just-in-Time, with daily kaizen by all employees; it credits Sakichi Toyoda's automatic looms (1896, 1924) for jidoka, Kiichiro Toyoda for Just-in-Time, and Eiji Toyoda and Taiichi Ohno for building TPS [43]. Wikipedia dates Ohno and Eiji Toyoda's development of TPS to 1948-1975 [44].
- Set-based concurrent engineering (SBCE): teams consider sets of solutions in parallel and narrow gradually. The term was coined by Ward et al. in 1994 for Toyota's practice; the "Second Toyota Paradox" (1995) is its precursor; MIT SMR says Toyota was an industry leader in development lead time with fewer engineers than U.S. rivals [45][47]. The three principles themselves were not readable.
- Sobek, Liker and Ward (HBR 1998) group Toyota's practices into six organisational mechanisms (publisher description, not opened) [46]; the chief-engineer (shusa) role is described in later work by Morgan and Liker (2006) (snippet only).
- *Inference:* TPS is a production system; the documented innovation content in these sources lies in the product-development system (SBCE, chief engineer), not the shop floor. Toyota's page itself describes TPS as aimed at eliminating waste and shortening lead times [43].

### What worked
- The Prius shows exploratory search inside Toyota's engineering culture [49]. Evidence that SBCE produces better or faster cars is thinner than often assumed: the 2020 Cambridge review says set-based design has "relatively low theoretical development" and cites a survey of 92 Japanese and 119 U.S. suppliers (set-based communication more common in Japan), plus simulations and real-options models, with no direct quantified Toyota performance data [47].

### Failure modes / criticisms
- 2009-11 recalls: about 9 million vehicles per Wikipedia and about 8.5 million per Al Jazeera; the causes were floor-mat pedal entrapment and sticking accelerator pedals, plus a hybrid brake-software recall. A NASA/NHTSA inquiry reported in February 2011 found no electronic faults that would cause the acceleration. Akio Toyoda told Congress in February 2010 that "the pace at which we have grown may have been too quick" [50][51]. *Inference:* this shows limits during rapid scale-up, not a failure of SBCE; no source links them.
- EV-lag critique: CNBC (2023) reported fewer than 25,000 battery EVs in a year (snippet only). Secondary 2025 data put Toyota/Lexus BEV sales at about 199,000 of about 10.5 million, under 2% [53] (snippet only, not Toyota's release). Toyota defends a multi-path strategy.
- Implementation outside Toyota is hard: a Kongsberg Automotive study examines pitfalls in adopting set-based design [54] (snippet only).

### Transferability
- *Inference:* SBCE suits hardware or integrated systems where late changes are costly and interfaces are clear; it conflicts with cultures that equate early convergence with progress. The limited quantitative evidence [47] argues for caution.

## Cross-company patterns

1. **Authority is explicit and singular, advice is plural.** Pixar's director, Amazon's single-threaded leader and Toyota's chief engineer each hold one accountable role while Braintrust, narrative reviews and option pools broaden input [4][25][45]. (Grouping is my inference. Amazon's single owner is a response to resource contention at scale, not an advice-versus-authority design [25].)
2. **Decision timing is engineered.** Pixar reviews work-in-progress [4]; Amazon writes the finished-state press release first [23][24]; Toyota delays convergence [45].
3. **Slack or optionality is protected structurally.** 3M's own-project time [28], Toyota's parallel options [45]. The 3M rule's specifics are not primary-verified.
4. **Shared knowledge assets.** 3M's platforms [28], Toyota's reuse of prior knowledge [47], AWS's origin in internal infrastructure pain [12].
5. **Each system came under a countervailing pressure.** Six Sigma and cost cutting at 3M [28], sequel/cost pressure and leadership change at Pixar [2][8][10], rapid growth at Toyota [51], and a founder-driven bet at Amazon [17]. These episodes are documented; causal attribution is inference and contested. Amazon is a partial exception: Bezos describes the Fire Phone failure as feeding Echo and Alexa [20], so failure there was redeployed rather than only suffered.
6. **Dependence on culture claims that are hard to test.** Most evidence is practitioner narrative; rigorous outcome studies were not found for Braintrust, 15% time or PR/FAQ. The only quasi-empirical item verified is the Benner and Tushman patent study cited by BusinessWeek [28].

## Lessons for modern operating models

1. **Separate advice from authority in creative or exploratory reviews.** Give reviewers voice but no veto, and name one accountable owner. [Evidence: moderate] (Pixar descriptions agree [4][5]; no outcome studies.)
2. **Write the customer outcome before building.** Use a short narrative or mock-announcement review to test the idea. [Evidence: weak] (Amazon says most major initiatives since 2004 used it [24], but the evidence of effect is testimonial and the Fire Phone shows it is insufficient [17].)
3. **Give separable, autonomous teams a dedicated, empowered leader when resource contention and shared-resource decisions slow a growing organisation.** [Evidence: weak] (Revised: the authors' own account cites resource contention and "learned helplessness" [25], and Jassy's letter gives the same logic [22]. The earlier "leader quality beats team size" finding was not supported.)
4. **Keep several options alive until learning justifies narrowing, when late change is expensive.** [Evidence: weak to moderate] (Downgraded: field studies since 1994 are interview and survey based, with limited direct performance data [47]; transfer beyond Toyota is difficult [54].)
5. **Protect exploratory slack, but pair it with sharing norms and platform reuse.** [Evidence: weak] (3M claims are descriptive; the policy's specifics and link to Post-it are not primary-verified.)
6. **Do not apply operational-efficiency metrics unchanged to exploratory R&D.** [Evidence: moderate] (Verified: BusinessWeek reports the new-product share falling from about one-third to one-quarter after Six Sigma, plus a patent study showing a shift to incremental work, and 3M leadership reversed course [28]. Causation is still contested, and R&D spending was flat, not cut, under McNerney.)
7. **Monitor for drift after growth, acquisition or cost pressure; set explicit portfolio guardrails (for example, originals vs. sequels).** [Evidence: inference] (Pixar [8][10], Toyota [51]; the guardrail idea is mine.)
8. **Measure innovation outcomes, not only inputs.** 3M's share of sales from products released in the last five years (the "vitality" idea) is the cited example. [Evidence: weak] (BusinessWeek confirms the one-third benchmark [28]; the index's predictive validity was not found.)
9. **Treat a failed bet as a source of reusable capability.** [Evidence: weak] (New: Bezos says Fire Phone learnings and developers accelerated Echo [20]; this is Amazon's own account and unaudited.)

## Open questions / contested claims

- **Post-it dates:** Fry's idea 1973 [29][34] vs 1974 [30][32][33]; Press 'n Peel test 1977 in four cities; Boise Blitz 1978; national launch April 1980 (Wikipedia's 3M page also says "Post-Its" were introduced in 1979). Whether Post-it was a product of 15% time specifically is asserted by secondary sources only [34][35]; no 3M primary was reachable.
- **When did the 15% rule start, and what are its conditions?** About 1948 per 3M (snippet) vs 1930s in a weak source; "no approval needed" and "must share findings" are unverified.
- **Platform count:** 45 (BusinessWeek 2007 [28]) vs 46 vs 49 (3M pages, snippet).
- **Six Sigma causality at 3M:** margin rise and new-product share fall are both documented [28]; causal link is the journalist's inference.
- **AWS origins:** 2002 APIs vs 2004 SQS vs 2006 S3/EC2 [12][13][14]; EC2 2006 beta vs 2008.
- **Two-pizza team origin:** not in the 2016-2021 letters; earlier letters unchecked; book unread.
- **Six-page memo origin (2004 email):** unverified.
- **Prius lead and design count:** Uchiyamada vs Yaegashi; 80 vs over 100 designs [48][49].
- **Recall count:** 8.5 vs 9 million [50][51].
- **Does the Braintrust scale or survive leadership change?** Reports of failed replication [6] are anecdotal; the Lasseter departure (2018) is documented [2], but its effect on the Braintrust was not studied.
- **Toyota's EV position** changes quickly; the 2025 figures are secondary.
- Not covered: HBS case studies, Amazon letters before 2016, Catmull's book text, Bryar and Carr's text. Next step: obtain these directly.
