## Snapshot (founded, parent, size, budget, staff, status today)

- **DARPA:** created 7 Feb 1958 as ARPA after Sputnik; "Defense" added in 1972 [24]. It sits inside the U.S. Department of Defense [1]. Roughly 200+ government employees including almost 100 program managers (PMs), overseeing about $3B a year (2018-19 CRS edition) [1][2]. CRS reported $3.50B in FY2021 (constant dollars), 2.4% below FY1996, i.e. broadly flat over decades [1]. For FY2026, sources conflict: one reports a ~$4.5B RDT&E request with $350M contingent on reconciliation [27]; another trade source gives much smaller figures that appear to be a different scope [search result: dsm.forecastinternational.com, not cited]. Current director Stephen Winchell was sworn in 20 May 2025 as the 24th director [26].
- **ARPA-E (DOE):** authorized by the 2007 America COMPETES Act; first funded FY2009 with $15M regular plus $400M Recovery Act money; FY2015 enacted $280M [11]. By March 2021: $2.6B to over 1,000 projects, 177 of which drew over $4.9B in private follow-on funding (ITIF figures) [12].
- **IARPA (ODNI):** authorized 2006; operations began 1 Oct 2007 under founding director Lisa Porter; formed by merging three existing intelligence technology offices [14].
- **ARPA-H (HHS):** structure set in 2022, placed within NIH with its director reporting to the HHS Secretary; first director Renee Wegrzyn from Sept 2022 [15]. A source reports $1B at launch and about $2.5B by May 2023 [15 (search summary)].
- **ARIA (UK):** established 26 Jan 2023 under the Advanced Research and Invention Agency Act 2022; roughly £800M earmarked, with a further £1B+ reported for 2025-29; a secondary source reports 53 staff and a £27.6M operating budget in FY2024/25 [19]. Programme Directors each steer about £50M [18].

## Operating model

- **PM-centered:** about one hundred PMs, plus office and agency leadership, form the core [6]. PMs are hired for limited terms, usually three to five years, via special hiring authorities [6][1].
- **Outsourced R&D:** DARPA runs no labs; it contracts with industry, universities, nonprofits and federal labs [1].
- **Program shape:** specific technical goals, budgets of tens of millions, three to five years [6 (search summary)].
- **Heilmeier Catechism:** questions credited to George Heilmeier (director 1975-77) used to vet programs: plain-language objectives, current practice and its limits, what is new, who cares, risks and payoffs, cost, time, and midterm/final "exams" [3][4][5]. DARPA lists eight; some secondary versions say nine, and ARPA-H adds an equity question [3][4].
- **Flexible authorities:** Congress gave DARPA special acquisition and hiring authorities [1]. DARPA's Other Transaction (OT) authority dates to 1989 and sits outside standard contracts, grants and cooperative agreements [25 (search summary)]. IPA and other term-hire mechanisms are asserted in the literature [6] but their legal detail was not verified here.
- **Azoulay et al. synthesis:** the ARPA model rests on administrative flexibility plus PMs with wide authority to design programs, select projects and actively manage them, aimed at mission-oriented work on nascent technology S-curves in inefficient innovation systems [8].
- **Pasteur's quadrant:** DARPA is commonly described as operating in use-inspired basic research [search result summary of ISR Michigan, not independently cited].
- **Ending programs:** [unverified] DARPA staff say programs are stopped when milestones fail; I did not see a primary source.
- **Hand-off:** work must be passed to services or industry; see transition critique below [23].
- **Fuchs's finding:** from a case study of the Microsystems Technology Office, 1992-2008, informal institutions among PMs, not just formal structure, sustained DARPA through the Tether era [7].

## What made it work (evidence-backed)

1. **Empowered, rotating PMs plus flexible authorities** are the two features Azoulay et al. identify as core [8]. Bonvillian et al. conclude that DARPA's success rests on features that evolved over time and were not all present at founding [6].
2. **Embedded networks:** Fuchs argues informal PM-to-PM and performer ties are a lasting asset [7].
3. **Stable scale:** funding broadly flat for decades suggests success did not require rapid growth [1]. (Inference.)
4. **Related evidence on funding style:** HHMI's tolerance of early failure and long horizons produced more high-impact papers than comparable NIH grantees [9]. This supports the principle of freedom plus tolerance for failure but is not evidence about DARPA itself (inference).

Caveat: there is no controlled evaluation of DARPA's counterfactual; one academic summary notes empirical evaluation of ARPA-style agencies remains elusive [14 search summary / UChicago reader].

## Failure modes and criticisms

- **Transition / valley of death:** GAO (2015) found DARPA's transition tracking unreliable; DOD lacks a common definition of "transition," and DARPA counted a still-in-development biofouling project as one. DARPA partly disagreed [23]. Commentators attribute the gap to requirements and multi-year budget cycles (PPBE) rather than DARPA alone [search summaries, NPS/AcquisitionTalk, not independently cited].
- **ARPA-E:** National Academies (2017) found it was meeting its statutory mission, noted a quarter of ~500 projects had drawn follow-on private funding, and urged a reconceived technology-to-market effort [10]. A House Science staff report, citing GAO, found 18 of 44 small/medium awardees had earlier private investment for similar technology, and the committee framed the record as mixed [13]. Political survival was in doubt when the 2018 budget request proposed ending it [10 search summary]. Note that the Azoulay authors also served on the Academies review [8 search summary], so the two are not independent.
- **IARPA:** modified model with independent testing and evaluation [14]; I found no independent outcomes evaluation.
- **ARPA-H:** inaugural director removed in Feb 2025, more than a year before the end of her term [16]. FY26 cuts of up to 37% were proposed but Congress kept funding flat [17]. Too early for outcome evidence.
- **ARIA:** no outcome evidence yet; a slow start was reported (formal establishment Jan 2023) [19].
- **Model transfer:** Bonvillian et al. ask which features of DARPA's environment contributed, noting other agencies and foreign governments have tried to copy it [6]. The model was modified, not cloned, in IARPA [6 search summary].

## Lessons for modern operating models

1. Pair PM authority with administrative flexibility; copying only one is unlikely to work. [Evidence: moderate] [8][6]
2. Use term-limited, outside-hired PMs and outsource R&D to performers. [Evidence: moderate; descriptive, not causally tested] [1][6]
3. Force clarity at program creation with Heilmeier-style questions and explicit interim milestones. [Evidence: weak to moderate; widely adopted, not tested] [3][4]
4. Design the hand-off before launch; transition capacity is where agencies are most criticized. [Evidence: moderate] [23][10]
5. Protect the agency from stop-start political shocks (leadership removal, budget swings). [Evidence: weak; two cases] [16][17]
6. Measure with pre-registered outcome metrics (follow-on funding, patents, adoption); current evidence leans on self-reported counts. [Evidence: inference]
7. Do not credit DARPA with whole technology lineages; the Moderna and Siri cases show partial roles. [Evidence: moderate] [20][21][22]

## Open questions / contested claims

- How much of DARPA's record is the model versus Cold War budgets and a large defense customer? [inference; 6 asks this]
- Does the model work where there is no single procurement customer (energy, health)? ARPA-E's mixed record [10][13] and ARPA-H's early turmoil [16] leave this open.
- Count of Heilmeier questions (eight vs nine) and ARPA-H's added questions [3][4].
- Moderna seedling grant size ($0.7M vs ~$1.4M) [20][21]; Siri funding totals [22].
- DARPA FY2026 budget figures conflict across sources [27].
- ARIA's FY2024/25 staff and budget numbers come from a secondary source [19].
- Stealth and Grand Challenge claims need a primary source.
