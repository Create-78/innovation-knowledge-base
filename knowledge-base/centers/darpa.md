## Snapshot (founded, parent, size, budget, staff, status today)

- **DARPA:** created 7 Feb 1958 as ARPA after Sputnik; renamed DARPA in 1972, back to ARPA in 1993 and to DARPA again in 1996 [1][11]. It sits inside the U.S. Department of Defense [1]. CRS (2021 edition) says more than 200 government employees, including about 100 program managers (PMs) [1]; Bonvillian et al. say "approximately one hundred" PMs plus office and agency leaders [2]. Wikipedia's 2026 text gives about 1,122 government employees and about 250 programs; I could not tie the 1,122 figure to a primary source and it conflicts with CRS, so treat it as unverified [11]. Budget: CRS reports $3.50B in FY2021 (constant dollars), 2.4% below FY1996 ($3.59B), a long-run average of $3.34B [1]. DARPA's own site states the FY2026 enacted budget was $4.322B and the FY2027 request is $5.039B [6]. DARPA lists six technical offices; its site now names them Biological Technologies, Defense Sciences, Information Processing Techniques, "Multi X", Strategic Technology and Tactical Technology, i.e. the former Information Innovation and Microsystems offices appear to have been renamed or restructured (reason not verified) [6]. Director Stephen Winchell was sworn in 20 May 2025 as the 24th director, after leading AI and autonomy at the Strategic Capabilities Office [7]; Rob McHenry was acting director from 20 Jan 2025 [11].
- **ARPA-E (DOE):** created by the 2007 America COMPETES Act without money; the first funding was about $400M of Recovery Act money in 2009; the 2010 reauthorization changed its structure (42 U.S.C. 16538) [15]. As of 2026 Wikipedia reports over 1,000 projects, $2.6B invested, and about $4.9B in private follow-on money tied to 179 projects (an ITIF 2021 figure said 177) [15][27]. Directors: Majumdar, Williams, Genatowski, Wang; Conner Prochaska confirmed 18 Sept 2025 [15].
- **IARPA (ODNI):** authorized 2006; operations began 1 Oct 2007; formed by merging NSA's Disruptive Technology Office, NGA's National Technology Alliance and CIA's Intelligence Technology Innovation Center. Lisa Porter was the first director (Wikipedia dates her 2008-2012). Russell Miller has been director since Dec 2025, after Rick Muller (Apr 2024-Dec 2025) [16].
- **ARPA-H (HHS):** created by the Consolidated Appropriations Act 2022 (P.L. 117-103, 15 Mar 2022); located in HHS using NIH resources, reporting to the HHS Secretary; first director Renee Wegrzyn from Sept 2022 [17]. Funding: $1B initial (FY2022), $1.5B (FY2023), and $1.5B in FY2025 [17][search summary]. Wikipedia says FY2024 was $2.5B requested and received, which conflicts with the $1.5B pattern; unresolved. For FY2026 the White House asked for $945M and the House bill matched it, but a package signed 3 Feb 2026 reportedly kept $1.5B, and ARPA-H's FY2026 operating plan lists $1.5B [search summary; not opened]. Alicia Jackson was appointed director in Nov 2025 after Jason Roos served as acting director [17].
- **ARIA (UK):** established 26 Jan 2023 under the Advanced Research and Invention Agency Act 2022, a ten-year legal mandate [20]. About £800M was announced in 2021 [20][25]; at least £1B for 2025-29 was committed in 2025 [20]. In its first two years it approved seven programmes worth £471M and says its initial programmes make over £400M available over three to five years [19]. FY2024/25 net operating expenditure was £30.7M (R&D £16.5M, staff costs £6.4M, other operating £7.7M); grant-in-aid received was £31.5M [19]. Kathleen Fisher became CEO in Feb 2026, succeeding Ilan Gur; Matt Clifford is Chair [20].

## Operating model

- **PM-centered:** about one hundred PMs, plus office and agency leadership, form the core; most are government employees hired on a term basis, usually three to five years [2]. CRS says DARPA estimates 25% of PMs turn over annually and that PMs select and, if needed, terminate projects without being required to use peer review [1]. CRS also notes concerns about lost institutional memory and a recruitment process in which existing or former PMs identify new PMs [1].
- **Outsourced R&D:** DARPA runs no labs; it contracts with industry, universities, nonprofits and federal labs [1].
- **Program shape:** specific technical objectives, budgets of tens of millions of dollars, three to five years, often funding several performers on different technical approaches [2]. Bonvillian et al. stress that DARPA often returns to a technology over multiple generations, so short programs do not mean short horizons [2].
- **Heilmeier Catechism:** DARPA's page prints eight questions and attributes them to George H. Heilmeier, director 1975-77: objectives without jargon; how it is done today and the limits; what is new and why it should work; who cares; risks; cost; time; mid-term and final "exams" [5]. Some secondary versions say nine, and ARPA-H adds an equity question [26, not opened].
- **Flexible authorities (verified in CRS):** Other Transaction (OT) authority was first granted to DARPA in 1989 (P.L. 101-189, sec. 251); an OT is an acquisition instrument outside traditional contracts, grants and cooperative agreements and outside federal procurement regulations [1]. It was extended to the wider Department of Defense later; today research OTs rest on 10 U.S.C. 4021 and prototype OTs (with follow-on production) on 10 U.S.C. 4022 [8]. For hiring, Congress created an experimental authority in 1998 (P.L. 105-261) to hire outside science and engineering experts for limited terms; it was later modified (FY2017 NDAA, P.L. 114-328) and in 2019 the cap was raised from 100 to 140 hires (P.L. 116-92) [1]. IPA assignments and other term-hire routes are asserted in the literature but I did not verify their detail.
- **Azoulay et al. synthesis:** the ARPA model rests on administrative flexibility plus PMs with wide authority to design programs, select projects and actively manage them, aimed at mission-oriented work on nascent technology S-curves in inefficient innovation systems [4]. The paper is conceptual and does not report outcome estimates [4].
- **Pasteur's quadrant:** DARPA is commonly described as operating in use-inspired basic research [unverified; not seen in a source opened].
- **Ending programs:** CRS says projects are judged against milestones set by PMs in advance, and progress toward them decides whether continued funding is merited; funds can be redirected [1]. A secondary report attributes to Winchell that roughly 70% of DARPA programs do not meet their original metrics, which he does not call failure [search summary; unverified].
- **Hand-off:** DARPA's own about page says transition partners handle implementation, technology transfer and commercialization [6]; see the transition critique below.
- **Fuchs's finding:** a grounded-theory study of the Microsystems Technology Office's role in four semiconductor materials technologies, 1992-2008, finds that PMs' informal institutions, not just formal structure and culture, shaped technology trajectories, and that this persisted through director Tony Tether's 2001-08 changes, which drew an outcry that DARPA was no longer "the old DARPA" [3].

## What made it work (evidence-backed)

1. **Empowered, rotating PMs plus flexible authorities** are the two features Azoulay et al. identify as core [4]. Bonvillian et al. stress that DARPA "has evolved" and nearly closed on several occasions (for example the 1965-70 crisis), so its features were not all present at founding [2].
2. **Embedded networks:** Fuchs argues informal PM-to-PM and performer ties are a lasting asset [3].
3. **Stable scale:** funding broadly flat in real terms for 25 years (FY1996-FY2021) suggests success did not require rapid growth [1]. The FY2026 enacted level of $4.322B [6] is higher than the CRS series; the mix of scope changes was not checked. (Inference.)
4. **Related evidence on funding style:** HHMI's tolerance of early failure and long horizons produced more high-impact papers than comparable NIH grantees [24]. This supports the principle of freedom plus tolerance for failure but is not evidence about DARPA itself (inference).

Caveat: there is no controlled evaluation of DARPA's counterfactual; one academic summary notes empirical evaluation of ARPA-style agencies remains elusive [search summary, UChicago reader; not opened]. The Azoulay et al. paper is likewise conceptual [4].

## Failure modes and criticisms

- **Transition / valley of death:** GAO-16-5 (18 Nov 2015) reviewed about 150 programs completed FY2010-14. It found DARPA's definitions and assessments of transition inconsistent, so GAO could not reliably report portfolio-wide transition performance; DOD has no standard definition of transition. DARPA counted a biofouling project as a transition because it moved to follow-on development; GAO classed it a non-transition because funding was terminated. GAO found demand and a link to an area of sustained DARPA interest were the main success factors, that DARPA largely opted out of DOD transition programs, and that transition falls to PMs with limited training. DOD partially concurred; GAO closed all three recommendations in 2018 as "Closed - Not Implemented" because DARPA reported no changes [9][10]. CRS adds that GAO found the Air Force initially resisted stealth, and that DOD's growing use of prototype OTs could help fill the maturation-funding gap [1]. Commentators also blame requirements and PPBE cycles rather than DARPA alone [search summaries; not opened].
- **ARPA-E:** the National Academies (2017) found its projects support its statutory mission but that impact is hard to measure and the agency was young; its metrics showed 45 projects with over $1.25B in private follow-on funding and 36 new companies as of the 2016 summit, out of about 475 projects in its comparison (my arithmetic: under 10%). An earlier version of this profile said "a quarter"; I could not find that statement in the chapter [14]. A 2012 House Science majority staff report, citing GAO, found 18 of 44 small/medium awardees had earlier private investment for similar technology [28, not opened]. The 2018 budget request proposing to end ARPA-E is a search-summary claim [unverified]. All four Azoulay et al. authors took part in the Academies review, so the two are not independent [4].
- **IARPA:** the ARPA model with independent testing and evaluation and shared metrics across competing teams [16]; I found no independent outcomes evaluation.
- **ARPA-H:** Wegrzyn was fired 14 Feb 2025, more than a year before her four-year term ended, the day after RFK Jr. was confirmed as HHS Secretary amid layoffs across health agencies [18]. The FY2026 cut of up to 37% proposed by the White House was not enacted; $1.5B was reportedly kept, though at least 15 ARPA-H staff were reportedly laid off the same week [search summary; unverified]. Too early for outcome evidence.
- **ARIA:** no outcome evidence yet. Reported frictions: exemption from Freedom of Information requests, a 2026 Guardian report on grants to US technology and venture firms, and objections to its outdoor geoengineering experiments [20, Wikipedia summary of press reports]. First-year spend (£30.7M, of which £16.5M R&D) was small relative to the £471M committed [19].
- **Model transfer:** Bonvillian et al. note other agencies and foreign governments have tried to copy it and ask which environmental features mattered [2]; IARPA modified rather than cloned it [16].

## Lessons for modern operating models

1. Pair PM authority with administrative flexibility; copying only one is unlikely to work. [Evidence: moderate] [4][2]
2. Use term-limited, outside-hired PMs (3-5 years, about 25% annual turnover) and outsource R&D to performers; accept the cost in institutional memory. [Evidence: moderate; descriptive, not causally tested] [1][2]
3. Force clarity at program creation with Heilmeier-style questions and explicit interim milestones. [Evidence: weak to moderate; widely adopted, not tested] [5][1]
4. Design the hand-off before launch; transition capacity is where agencies are most criticized, and GAO's 2015 recommendations went unimplemented. [Evidence: moderate] [9][1][14]
5. Protect the agency from stop-start political shocks (leadership removal, budget swings). [Evidence: weak; two cases] [18][17]
6. Measure with pre-registered outcome metrics (follow-on funding, patents, adoption); current evidence leans on self-reported counts, and DOD has no common definition of transition. [Evidence: moderate] [9][14]
7. Do not credit DARPA with whole technology lineages; Moderna, Siri and stealth show early-funder roles with others (Penn, SRI, Skunk Works, the Air Force) carrying the build or fielding. [Evidence: moderate] [21][22][13]

## Open questions / contested claims

- How much of DARPA's record is the model versus Cold War budgets and a large defense customer? [inference; 2 asks this]
- Does the model work where there is no single procurement customer (energy, health)? ARPA-E's mixed record [14] and ARPA-H's turmoil [18] leave this open.
- Heilmeier question count: DARPA prints eight [5]; the ARPA-H added equity question is not re-checked.
- Moderna: seedling grant $0.7M [21] versus ~$1.4M implied elsewhere [23].
- DARPA headcount: CRS "200+" versus Wikipedia's 1,122 [1][11]. ARPA-H FY2024 funding: $1.5B or $2.5B.
- Siri funding totals [22].
- What the 2026 DARPA office renamings mean for the model [6].
