# Research Literature on How to Organize R&D and Innovation

Theory and evidence layer for the innovation knowledge base. Scope: 25 bodies of work on funding, team design, culture, portfolio process and commercialization. Compiled 2026-10-27 from web searches, then upgraded the same day by opening primary pages (see provenance).

**Provenance and verification.** The first draft used search snippets only. In a second pass with outbound access, these were opened and checked against the text: the full text of Azoulay, Graff Zivin and Manso (NBER w15466 PDF); NBER abstract pages for w21788, w24674, w20902 and w6725; the abstracts (publisher pages or Crossref records) of Wuchty et al. 2007, Edmondson 1999, Arora et al. 2018 (SMJ), Cooper 2008, Sethi and Iqbal 2008, Azoulay et al. 2011 (RAND), Bloom et al. 2020 (AER), Jones 2009 (SSRN version), Dushnitsky and Lenox 2005, Watzinger et al. 2020 (AEJ:Policy), Li and Agha 2015 (Science summary), Wu, Wang and Evans 2019 (RePEc), Park, Leahey and Funk (arXiv and Nature record); the King and Baatartogtokh SMR page; the Caltech Heilmeier page; and the Wikipedia Allen curve article. Bonvillian and Van Atta's journal record has no abstract and the full text is paywalled. **Not opened** (still search-snippet or catalog level): Stokes, Nielsen and Qiu, Amabile, Christensen, Lepore, Chesbrough, O'Reilly and Tushman, McKinsey Three Horizons, Clancy, the Guzey critique, the critiques of Park et al., Mansfield 1977 (figures from a secondary essay), Gompers 2002, Goldstein and Kearney 2020 and the Azoulay et al. NIH-patenting paper (secondary summary). Items needing full-text confirmation are flagged "(unverified detail)". Where a source is a secondary summary, that is stated.

## How to read this (evidence grading key)

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **A** | Large-sample or quasi-experimental causal design, peer-reviewed, with published critiques or replication attempts that largely survive. |
| **B** | Large-sample observational or correlational evidence, peer-reviewed. Strong descriptive pattern, causal interpretation is contested. |
| **C** | Small-N field, case or interview evidence, or conceptual frameworks backed by cases. Useful for hypotheses, weak for effect sizes. |
| **D** | Practitioner heuristic or essay. Influential but largely untested. |

A "confidence in implication" judgment is separate: a grade-B finding can still justify a design choice if the cost of being wrong is low.

## Findings by theme

### 1. Funding and selection

**Stokes, *Pasteur's Quadrant* (1997).** Stokes (Princeton) challenges the Vannevar Bush "linear model" that treats basic and applied science as separate, using Pasteur to show that the goals of understanding and use can coexist in one researcher [1].
- Evidence: C/D. It is a conceptual and historical argument, and the catalog records give no empirical test [1].
- Limits: it is a classification scheme, not a causal estimate of what organization performs better.
- Implication: do not force a basic-versus-applied org chart. Reserve explicit space for use-inspired basic research, with success judged on both understanding and use.

**Azoulay, Graff Zivin and Manso, HHMI vs NIH (2009 NBER; *RAND J. Econ.* 2011).** Using propensity-score weighting and difference-in-differences, they find HHMI investigators produce high-impact papers at a much higher rate than similarly accomplished NIH-funded scientists. Their research direction also shifts toward novel lines of inquiry [2][40]. HHMI is described as tolerating early failure and rewarding long-term success, versus NIH's short review cycles and renewal policies that are unforgiving of failure [2].
- Design (from the NBER full text): 73 HHMI investigators appointed in 1993-95, compared mainly with NIH-funded early career prize winners (ECPWs) chosen to match on the factors that drive HHMI selection. After dropping 49 scientists outside common support, the main sample is 69 HHMI and 348 controls (417 scientists). Outcomes are tracked pre-appointment (1986-94) and post-appointment (1998-2006) [2].
- Effect sizes (preferred estimates vs ECPWs): about +39% in publications, +55% in top-5% cited papers, and about +96-97% in top-1% cited papers. The effect grows toward the upper tail. HHMI scientists also "flop" more: 35% more papers below the citation level of their weakest pre-appointment work [2].
- Evidence: B+. It is a matched quasi-experimental design on a selected program. The authors state there is no exogenous variation in HHMI appointment and identification rests on selection on observables. Output divergence appears only 4-5 years after appointment [2].
- Correction: an earlier draft cited "roughly 2x top-5% and 3x top-1%" from a blog. The paper's figures are about 1.55x and about 1.96x, so those press numbers were overstated.
- Implication: long time horizons, tolerance for early failure and people-not-projects funding plausibly raise the rate of high-impact, novel work. Use as a design hypothesis for pilots, not a guaranteed multiplier.

**Azoulay, Fons-Rosen and Graff Zivin, "Does Science Advance One Funeral at a Time?" (NBER w21788).** Following 452 academic life scientists who died prematurely, collaborators' output in the field falls sharply and non-collaborators' output rises by 8.6% on average. The new entrants' papers are disproportionately highly cited and more often come from researchers not previously active in the field. Entry is limited by intellectual, social and resource barriers, and outsiders enter mainly subfields less hostile to unfamiliar ideas [3]. Published in *American Economic Review* 109(8), 2019 [3].
- Evidence: B+ (event-study design). Its relevance here is that incumbents' control of resources can gate new ideas.
- Implication: build in turnover, outsider access to resources and sunset rules for programs and leaders.

**Azoulay, Fuchs, Goldstein and Kearney, "Funding Breakthrough Research: Promises and Challenges of the 'ARPA Model'" (NBER w24674; *Innovation Policy and the Economy* v19).** They trace the model to 1958 and identify its core features as administrative flexibility and substantial authority for program directors to design programs, pick projects and actively manage them. They place its domain in mission-oriented research on emerging-technology S-curves within an inefficient innovation system [4]. The abstract also discusses implementation challenges and places ARPA among other approaches to research funding [4].
- Evidence: C/B-. It is a conceptual and institutional analysis, not a controlled comparison. The abstract states no effect sizes. The authors took part in the National Academies review of ARPA-E (not re-checked) and the working paper is not peer reviewed [4].
- Implication: if you adopt the ARPA pattern, protect program-manager authority and administrative flexibility. These are the model's identified active ingredients, not the label.

**Bonvillian and Van Atta, "ARPA-E and DARPA: Applying the DARPA model to energy innovation" (*J. Technology Transfer* 36(5), 2011).** The paper applies the DARPA model to ARPA-E and, per the book-chapter version, discusses the model, ARPA-E as a new DOE R&D model, and "the remaining technology implementation challenge" [5][6].
- Evidence: C (case-based, qualitative). Full text was not opened.
- Implication: the transition problem (moving results into adopters) is the model's weak point and needs its own design.

**Goldstein and Kearney, "Uncertainty and Individual Discretion in Allocating Research Funds" (SSRN 2017; NBER w32033, 2024).** Using internal ARPA-E project-selection data, they compare portfolios chosen by program-director discretion with those chosen by traditional peer review. Program directors tend to fund proposals with greater expert disagreement and ones reviewers call "creative." The 2024 abstract says this does not produce a significant tradeoff with short-term project performance [41].
- Evidence: B-/C. It is a descriptive analysis of one agency's internal data (the first quantitative study of its kind, per the authors), not a randomized comparison. Short-term performance is a weak proxy for breakthrough outcomes. Only the abstract was read; sample sizes not checked (unverified detail).
- Implication: this is the closest empirical support for the "empowered program director" ingredient in [4], and it supports tracking reviewer disagreement as a signal. A companion paper, Goldstein and Kearney, "Know when to fold 'em" (*Research Policy* 49(1), 2020), describes ARPA-E risk management; its abstract was not read [42].

**Li and Agha, "Big names or big ideas: Do peer-review panels select the best science proposals?" (*Science* 348(6233), 2015).** On more than 130,000 NIH grants funded 1980-2008 (137,215 R01s per a secondary summary), better peer-review scores predict more publications, citations and patents, even with controls for investigator track record, institution and career stage. A one-standard-deviation worse score goes with about 15% fewer citations (secondary summary figure, unverified detail) [43].
- Evidence: B. Large-sample observational. It observes only funded grants, so it cannot show whether peer review rejects high-potential proposals. An NHLBI commentary on cardiovascular R01s found peer-review rank discriminated poorly for top-10% papers (AUC 0.52) [43].
- Implication: peer review has real signal on average, so ARPA-style discretion should be tested against it, not assumed superior. The two literatures differ in outcome measure (short-term milestones vs citations).

**Azoulay, Graff Zivin, Li and Sampat, "Public R&D Investments and Private-sector Patenting: Evidence from NIH Funding Rules" (*Rev. Econ. Studies* 86(1), 2019).** Using rigidities in NIH peer-review rules for exogenous variation, they estimate that a $10 million NIH funding boost produces a net increase of about 2.7 patents (one draft reports 2.3) [44].
- Evidence: A-/B+ for the identification, but the figures come from a search summary, not the paper (unverified detail).
- Implication: public research funds spill into private patenting; relevant to partnering and buy-or-partner decisions in section 5.

**Heilmeier Catechism.** A set of questions used by George Heilmeier, DARPA director 1975-77, to evaluate proposed programs. They cover objectives in plain language, current practice and its limits, what is new, who cares, risks and payoffs, cost, time and milestones for success [7][8]. The Caltech page lists seven questions and calls the attribution "purported"; other sources give eight, nine, or ten in the ARPA-H adaptation, so cite the exact version you use [7][8].
- Evidence: D. No outcome evaluation was found.
- Implication: use as a low-cost screening and communication discipline at proposal intake. Do not treat it as evidence that screening improves outcomes.

**Nielsen and Qiu, "A Vision of Metascience."** An essay arguing that metascience needs three intertwined parts: a design practice, an entrepreneurial discipline and a research field. It treats funding, review and publishing as areas open to redesign [9]. The summary comes from a discussion-forum listing, not the essay text.
- Evidence: D (essay).
- Implication: treat the organization's own funding and review mechanisms as experimentable. Run small, measured trials, for example variant selection rules.

### 2. Team and structure

**Wuchty, Jones and Uzzi, *Science* 316 (2007).** Using 19.9 million papers over five decades and 2.1 million patents, they show teams increasingly dominate solo authors across nearly all fields and typically produce more frequently cited work, with the advantage growing over time. Teams now also produce the exceptionally high-impact work, even where solo authors once did [10][45].
- Evidence: B (large-sample, correlational). Team formation is endogenous: better projects may attract bigger teams.

**Jones, "The Burden of Knowledge and the Death of the Renaissance Man" (*Rev. Econ. Studies* 76(1), 2009).** A formal model with six testable predictions: if knowledge accumulates, successive innovators face a growing educational burden, leading to more schooling, narrower specialization and more teamwork. The author tests the predictions on a micro-data set of individual inventors and reports evidence consistent with each (SSRN version abstract) [11][46].
- Evidence: B- (theory plus supporting patterns). The earlier draft cited a Wikipedia overview; the abstract was checked in this pass. The "rising age of major contributions" attribution came from the overview and is not in the abstract (unverified detail).
- Implication: expect to need larger, more specialized and older teams over time. Budget for onboarding and for cross-field integration roles.

**Wu, Wang and Evans, "Large teams develop and small teams disrupt science and technology" (*Nature* 566, 2019).** Analyzing over 65 million papers, patents and software products (1954-2014), they find smaller teams tend to disrupt and larger teams tend to develop existing ideas. Larger teams build on recent, popular work and get attention quickly; smaller teams draw on older work and may take longer to be recognized, or never are. Differences are stronger for high-impact work, topic and research design explain only a small part, and most of the effect comes from individuals moving between small and large teams [12]. They argue for supporting a diversity of team sizes [12].
- Evidence: B (large-sample, uses a citation-based disruption measure; individual-level mobility analysis strengthens it).
- Limits: see the contested section for the disruption-index debate [13][14].
- Implication: allocate explicit capacity to small exploratory teams and large development teams. Do not scale a team just because the project grows.

**Allen curve (Thomas Allen, *Managing the Flow of Technology*).** The frequency of technical communication falls with distance between engineers' workstations, with a critical distance around 50 meters cited [15].
- Evidence: C/B- (1970s-80s studies of R&D labs). Wikipedia dates the research to the late 1970s and the book to 1984, and says Allen reexamined it with later data and found communication across all media still decays with distance, though the article's own citation for this is a 2006 book, so the 2007 date is unclear [15]. A blog states the 50-meter number is hard to trace to its origin, and publication dates are reported inconsistently (1977 vs 1984) [15][16]. I did not open Allen's book.
- Implication: co-locate teams that must exchange tacit knowledge frequently. Treat the 50 m figure as a heuristic, and for remote work compensate with deliberate communication design.

**Tushman/O'Reilly ambidexterity (see Portfolio).** Structural separation of exploratory units from core is the main team-structure prescription [17].

### 3. Talent and culture

**Amabile, intrinsic motivation and progress.** Amabile's early work found people are more creative when doing something interesting, challenging and personally satisfying. Her later work covers team creativity and organizational innovation [18]. In *The Progress Principle* (with Kramer, 2011) the evidence is diary data: stories from 238 white-collar employees at seven companies, nearly 12,000 diary entries. The headline claim is that a sense of progress on meaningful work is the most important factor [18].
- Evidence: B- for the intrinsic motivation work, C for the diary study (observational, self-report, small number of firms).
- Limits: the *Creativity in Context* model itself was not retrieved, so I cannot confirm its specification (unverified detail).
- Implication: protect meaningful goals and visible progress. Remove obstacles and avoid heavy extrinsic-control mechanisms for exploratory work.

**Edmondson, "Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams" (*Admin. Sci. Quarterly* 44(2), 1999).** Team psychological safety is a shared belief that interpersonal risk-taking is acceptable. In a multimethod field study of 51 work teams in one manufacturing company, safety was associated with learning behavior, while team efficacy was not once safety was controlled. Learning behavior mediated between safety and team performance [19].
- Evidence: B-/C (small N, single company, cross-sectional; the abstract reports a multimethod field study).
- Implication: measure psychological safety with a survey in project teams, and treat low scores as a leading indicator for learning and failure-reporting problems.

**Azoulay et al. on HHMI** also speaks to culture: freedom to experiment and tolerance of failure are the mechanisms proposed [2].

### 4. Portfolio and process

**Christensen, *The Innovator's Dilemma* (1997).** Built on a study of the disk-drive industry, it argues established firms lead in sustaining innovations but tend to fail on disruptive ones [20][21].
- Evidence: C/B- (historical case study with a single main industry).
- Critiques: King and Baatartogtokh ("How Useful Is the Theory of Disruptive Innovation?", *MIT Sloan Management Review*, Fall 2015) used 79 industry experts to assess 77 cases cited by Christensen and Raynor and concluded many did not fit four key elements of the theory well. The page I opened does not discuss the disk-drive case [22]. The recommendation to use the best parts alongside classical strategy analysis is from the earlier draft and was not re-confirmed. Lepore (*New Yorker*, June 23, 2014) called the theory faulty and dangerous and questioned its evidence [23][24]. A 2006 HKUST thesis re-examined the 1986-88 disk-drive data and found the arguments unconvincing [21]. Christensen-aligned responses (Deloitte) argued Lepore mischarged the theory [24]. Another commentator said she was "on to something" but argued poorly [25].
- Implication: do not treat "spin out a separate unit" as proven. The theory is a useful lens for asking whether a threat targets low-end or new markets, but its predictive claims are disputed. The separate-unit prescription is also from the book; its text was not retrieved (unverified detail).

**O'Reilly and Tushman, "Organizational Ambidexterity: Past, Present, and Future" (Stanford GSB working paper, 2013; AMP version not confirmed).** Ambidexterity is an organization's capacity to both explore and exploit, competing in mature markets while entering new technologies and markets [17]. A secondary blog says the literature links it to sales growth, innovation, market valuation and survival, though it can be duplicative and inefficient [26].
- Evidence: B-/C. Mostly cross-sectional firm studies and cases; the summary comes from secondary sources.
- Implication: use structural ambidexterity (separate exploratory unit, senior integration) as one option, and have leadership explicitly arbitrate resource conflicts. Test the costs of duplication.

**Chesbrough, *Open Innovation* (HBS Press, 2003).** Argues that the internally focused innovation model is becoming obsolete and that firms should use internal and external ideas and multiple paths to market. Case studies include Xerox, IBM, Lucent, Intel, Merck and Millennium [27][28].
- Evidence: C/D. The book is semi-academic, summarizing work published elsewhere and aimed at managers [28].
- Implication: build scouting, licensing-in, partnerships and out-licensing into the operating model. Expect cherry-picked cases in the literature, and track your own hit rates.

**Cooper, Stage-Gate (*J. Product Innovation Mgmt* 25, 2008).** The article describes how the system is meant to work, corrects the myths that it is linear or rigid, and identifies governance problems, over-bureaucratization and misuse of cost-cutting methods such as Six Sigma and Lean in product innovation [29].
- Evidence: C/D. The author is the method's originator and the cited basis is his benchmarking studies, which I did not retrieve. The abstract also offers remedies ("gates with teeth," defined gatekeepers, leaner gates) and scalable, more flexible next-generation versions [29]. A secondary summary says execution quality, not just process, separates winners from losers [30]; I could not verify that against the original.
- Critique: Cooper himself reports over-bureaucratization [29]. An academic critique (Sethi and Iqbal, *J. Marketing* 72(1), 2008; the earlier draft wrongly gave 71(1), 2007) surveyed 120 Stage-Gate projects. Strictly enforced, objective gate criteria made projects more inflexible, which led to learning failure, and this hurt the market performance of novel products, more so in turbulent technology environments. Gate conditionality did not mitigate it [31].
- Evidence for the critique: B-/C (cross-sectional survey of one process, abstract read only).
- Implication: use gates for incremental, well-defined development. Use lighter staging with tolerance for learning and pivoting for exploratory work.

**McKinsey Three Horizons (Baghai, Coley and White, *The Alchemy of Growth*).** Horizon 1 is core businesses, Horizon 2 is emerging opportunities, Horizon 3 is ideas for profitable growth down the road. The horizons are not sequential stages and must be managed concurrently [32][33].
- Evidence: D. Based on consultant research at high-performing companies [33]; no controlled test found.
- Implication: use it as a portfolio vocabulary. Assign each project a horizon, with distinct metrics and governance per horizon.

**Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen and Webb, "Are Ideas Getting Harder to Find?" (*AER* 110(4), 2020).** Research effort has grown sharply while output per researcher has fallen. The abstract says the number of researchers needed today to achieve the Moore's Law doubling of chip density is "more than 18 times larger" than in the early 1970s, with evidence from "various industries, products, and firms" [34].
- Evidence: B. It uses aggregate and firm-level measures with strong modeling assumptions.
- Critique: Guzey and coauthors argue the paper does not adequately support its conclusions, particularly through its use of TFP as a measure of innovation [35] (critique not opened). The earlier draft claimed the preliminary version said "more than 75 times"; the published AER page does not mention 75, and I did not open a preliminary version, so treat that claim as unverified [34].
- Implication: plan on rising cost per breakthrough in mature fields. Favor newer or less-mined areas and measure productivity per researcher, not just output.

### 5. Commercialization and transition

**Arora, Belenzon and Patacconi, "The decline of science in corporate R&D" (*Strategic Mgmt J.* 39(1), 2018).** They show a shift away from science by large corporations between 1980 and 2006 (2007 in the NBER version, titled "Killing the Golden Goose?"). Company scientists' publications fell across industries and the market value tied to scientific research dropped, while the value of patented technical knowledge held steady. Trends are unlikely to be driven mainly by changes in publication practices, and science still helps innovation; the authors read this as a fall in the private returns to internal research: firms still value "the golden eggs" but not "the golden goose" [36][37]. The NBER version associates the shift with globalization and narrower firm scope [37]. The "US" restriction in the earlier draft is not in the abstracts I read.
- Evidence: B. Large-sample panel of firms, with the publication-practice artifact explicitly argued against [36].
- Implication: the "knowledge supply" role has migrated to universities and startups. Operating models should include buy-or-partner routes for science, with absorptive capacity to use it.

**Bonvillian and Van Atta** (above) frame transition as a core challenge for ARPA-style agencies [5][6].

**Chesbrough** (above) supplies multiple-routes-to-market logic [27].

**Watzinger, Fackler, Nagler and Schnitzer, "How Antitrust Enforcement Can Spur Innovation: Bell Labs and the 1956 Consent Decree" (*AEJ: Economic Policy* 12(4), 2020).** The 1956 decree forced Bell to license all its existing patents royalty-free, including those unrelated to telecommunications. Using exactly matched non-Bell patents as controls, the authors find a lasting increase in follow-on innovation building on Bell patents, but only in markets outside telecommunications [47]. Working-paper versions reported about +17% over the first five years, with most of the gain from small and young firms and individual inventors (secondary summaries, unverified detail) [47].
- Evidence: A-/B+. A matched-control design on a single natural experiment; the effect is on follow-on citing patents, not on Bell Labs' own research productivity.
- Implication: the openness of a research lab's IP (compulsory licensing) is an important downstream channel. For a Bell Labs-style lab, the decree is evidence that licensing out raised outside innovation, not evidence about the lab's internal organization. It also suggests that licensing alone did not break foreclosure within telecoms.

**Mansfield, Rapoport, Romeo, Wagner and Beardsley, "Social and Private Rates of Return from Industrial Innovations" (*QJE* 91(2), 1977).** A case study of 17 industrial innovations. A secondary essay reports a median social return of about 56% against a median private return of about 25%, with private returns too low to justify investment for roughly a third of the cases [48].
- Evidence: C/B- (17 cases, selected, 1970s). I confirmed the citation but could not open the paper; the figures come from a secondary essay (unverified detail).
- Implication: the gap between social and private returns is the standing argument for public or patient funding of R&D. Treat the numbers as indicative only.

**Gompers and Lerner, "The Determinants of Corporate Venture Capital Success" (NBER w6725, 1998); Gompers, Atlanta Fed *Economic Review* (2002); Dushnitsky and Lenox, *SMJ* 26(10), 2005.** Gompers and Lerner analyzed over 30,000 transactions by corporate and other venture organizations. Corporate venture investments appear at least as successful as independent VC (for example, probability of the portfolio company going public), especially when parent and portfolio firm have strategic overlap. Corporate programs without strategic focus are much less stable and often stop after a few investments [49]. Dushnitsky and Lenox find firms invest more in new ventures in industries with weak IP protection and, to some extent, high technological ferment, and that greater cash flow and absorptive capacity raise the likelihood of investing [50].
- Evidence: B- (observational, 1990s US data; the 2002 summary claim that CVC beat independent VC was seen only in a search summary).
- Implication: if a corporate venturing arm is used as a window on outside science, give it strategic focus and a stable mandate. The literature is mixed on financial returns (a later meta-analysis was seen in a search summary only).

**Matt Clancy, New Things Under the Sun.** A "living literature review on the economics of innovation and science of science," written by Clancy, who is employed by Open Philanthropy, which funds the project but does not exercise editorial control [38][39]. Clancy says it publishes faster and with less hedging than academic norms, and is more provisional [38].
- Evidence: this is a meta-source (grade varies by post). I could not open or verify any individual post in this session, so no post-level claims are made.
- Implication: use it as an update feed for the knowledge base. Re-verify each post against the underlying papers before relying on it.

## Contested / failed-to-replicate findings

- **Disruptive-innovation theory.** King and Baatartogtokh found many of Christensen's cited cases did not fit key elements [22]. Lepore challenged the evidence and framing [23], and defenders replied [24]. No formal replication of the disk-drive result was found beyond a master's thesis [21]. Treat as a useful but non-predictive framework.
- **Disruption index trends.** Park, Leahey and Funk report papers and patents becoming less disruptive across fields [13]. Critics argued in *Nature* that part of the measured decline reflects database entries with no listed references, and the authors replied the decline remains large and significant [13][14]. The same CD-index logic underlies the Wu, Wang and Evans team-size result [12], so the measure's validity bears on both (inference from shared method).
- **"Ideas are getting harder to find."** Challenged on the use of TFP as the innovation measure [35]. The published abstract says "more than 18 times" [34]; the draft-to-publication change from 75x was not verified.
- **Allen curve's 50 m number.** Widely repeated, hard to trace to origin, with conflicting publication dates [15][16].
- **Stage-Gate.** Evidence is mostly from its originator. A peer-reviewed survey of 120 projects reports adverse effects on novel products [29][31].
- **HHMI result.** Identification is selection on observables with no exogenous variation in appointment, and divergence appears only years after appointment [2]. No independent replication was seen.
- **Cross-cutting caveat.** I found no direct replication results for Edmondson 1999, Amabile's model or the HHMI result in the sources I saw. The absence of a found replication is not evidence of replication.
